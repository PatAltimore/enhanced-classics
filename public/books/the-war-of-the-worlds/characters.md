---
title: "The War of the Worlds"
author: "H. G. Wells"
year: 1898
book_slug: "the-war-of-the-worlds"
license: "public-domain"

characters:
  - name: "The Narrator"
    role: "Unnamed philosophical writer from Maybury; the story's survivor"
    summary: "A middle-class writer on philosophical subjects who lives in a Woking suburb and watches the first Martian cylinder open. He sends his wife to Leatherhead, sees the tripods in a storm, hides in a ruined house with a curate, then walks through a dead London and finds the Martians dead. He is reunited with his wife in the last chapters."
    analysis: "The Narrator is an ordinary, thoughtful man whose civilised assumptions are repeatedly overturned, and his honest account, including the killing of the curate, gives the story its moral weight. He speaks for humanity's loss of confidence, and his reflections on rabbits, ants and dodos show Wells's central idea of a species dethroned."
  - name: "The Martians"
    role: "The invaders; brain-like creatures from a dying planet"
    summary: "Large, round, tentacled beings with no digestive system, who travel to Earth in ten cylinders and fight from tripod fighting-machines armed with the Heat-Ray and black smoke. They feed on human blood, clear land with steam jets and red weed, and nearly destroy London. They die of Earth's bacteria, to which they have no immunity."
    analysis: "The Martians are Wells's picture of intelligence without feeling, humanity's own future carried to an extreme. Their calm ruthlessness reflects the way Europeans treated colonised peoples and animals, and their sudden defeat by germs makes the book an ironic lesson about human weakness and the strength of nature."
  - name: "The Artilleryman"
    role: "Soldier survivor; a would-be saviour of the human race"
    summary: "A driver in the Horse Artillery whose gun is wiped out at Horsell Common. He meets the Narrator, then later again on Putney Hill, where he declares that humanity is beaten and lays out a plan to live underground in London's drains. He turns out to be lazy, and spends the day playing cards and drinking."
    analysis: "The Artilleryman is both a clear-eyed realist and a fantasist. His speeches on the Martians as masters and humans as eatable ants give the book its bleakest argument, while his failure to dig shows how little survival-minded rhetoric is worth. He is a satire on talk of heroic regeneration."
  - name: "The Curate"
    role: "Weybridge clergyman; the Narrator's companion in the ruined house"
    summary: "A young, fair, weak-chinned clergyman who is rescued by the Narrator after the Martians destroy his church. He raves about the wrath of God, refuses to act, and eats the food the two need. In the cellar he goes insane, shouts for the Martians and is struck down by the Narrator, then killed by a Martian."
    analysis: "The Curate shows how conventional religion fails when it is tested, and the Narrator's contempt for his helplessness, followed by remorse, is the moral core of the middle chapters. His insanity also puts a human face on the danger of fear, as his noise threatens to kill them both."
  - name: "The Narrator's Brother"
    role: "A medical student in London; his story of the exodus"
    summary: "A younger brother and medical student whose adventures are told in Book One. He follows the news from London, flees the panic of Monday morning, rescues two women from robbers, and travels with them to the Essex coast. He escapes by steamer, and watches the Thunder Child destroy two Martians before it is destroyed."
    analysis: "The brother gives the story a second viewpoint, showing the invasion as it affects a whole city and country, not just one witness. His narrative of the exodus, the crowd's greed and the sea battle widens the book from personal terror into a picture of civilisation in panic."
  - name: "The Narrator's Wife"
    role: "The Narrator's wife; the reason he wants to survive"
    summary: "A loving, anxious woman who is taken to Leatherhead by dog cart on the night the tripods first move. The Narrator believes she is dead after hearing that the town was destroyed. At the end she appears at their ruined house with her cousin, having come back to find him, and he catches her in his arms."
    analysis: "The wife is mostly absent, but her fate is the Narrator's main motive: his longing to find her gives him purpose when the world falls apart. Her reappearance is the book's quiet, human answer to its cosmic horror, and a reminder of what the Martians failed to destroy."
  - name: "Ogilvy"
    role: "Astronomer at Ottershaw; the first man to see the cylinder"
    summary: "A well-known astronomer who observes the flashes on Mars with the Narrator and scoffs at the idea of life there. He finds the cylinder on Horsell Common, realises that it is being unscrewed from within, and tries to alert the world. He dies in the first Heat-Ray attack, waving a white flag in the deputation."
    analysis: "Ogilvy stands for the optimism and curiosity of Victorian science, which cannot imagine an enemy beyond its models. His death among the first victims, marked by his attempt to talk with the Martians, reveals the uselessness of civilised signals against a stronger power."
  - name: "Miss Elphinstone"
    role: "The surgeon's sister-in-law who travels with the Narrator's brother"
    summary: "A calm, slender young woman who fights off robbers with a whip, produces a revolver and drives the pony in the lane. She helps the brother lead her sister-in-law through the panic on the Great North Road, and with him crosses the Essex coast to the steamer for Ostend."
    analysis: "Miss Elphinstone is a counterexample to the book's panic and the Victorian idea of women as helpless. Her coolness and courage, which Wells praises twice, make her one of the few characters who respond to the crisis with practical decision."
  - name: "The Thunder Child"
    role: "Torpedo ram; the one warship that attacks the Martians"
    summary: "A low iron ram that lies off the Essex coast, the only warship near the refugee fleet. As three Martians wade out towards the shipping, she steams at them without firing, destroys two with her guns and her own wreckage, and is lost to the Heat-Ray, letting the steamer escape."
    analysis: "The Thunder Child is the book's lone act of heroism, a human machine meeting Martian machines. Her charge, told by the Narrator's brother, shows courage in defeat, and her sinking confirms the Martians' superiority even as it saves lives."
