---
title: "The Time Machine"
author: "H.G. Wells"
year: 1895
book_slug: "the-time-machine"
license: "public-domain"

characters:
  - name: "The Time Traveller"
    role: "Inventor and protagonist; the story's central narrator"
    summary: "A gifted, argumentative scientist who builds a machine to travel through time and tells his friends an astonishing tale. He journeys to the year 802,701, where he loses his machine, befriends Weena, discovers the Morlocks and escapes only by chance. He returns, then sets off again and does not come back."
    analysis: "The Time Traveller is a man of Victorian confidence who learns how wrong his theories about the future can be. He first reads the Eloi's world as a utopia, then as a dystopia, so his changing explanations show the limits of reason. His disappearance leaves the story's moral open."
  - name: "The Narrator"
    role: "A guest at the dinner; the frame narrator"
    summary: "An unnamed guest who listens to the Time Traveller's story at dinner and retells it. He describes the Time Traveller's odd character, his late return in a dishevelled state, and his later disappearance. He watches the white flowers and wonders what has become of him."
    analysis: "The Narrator mediates between the story and the reader, voicing the sceptical and half-persuaded response. His ending, which imagines the Time Traveller in some far era, leaves the account both uncertain and moving, and his hope in the flowers shows a faith in human feeling."
  - name: "Weena"
    role: "A young Eloi woman who befriends the Time Traveller"
    summary: "An Eloi whom the Time Traveller rescues from drowning in the river. She becomes his companion, follows him about, and is frightened of the dark. During the fire in the wood she is lost, probably taken by the Morlocks, and he never sees her again."
    analysis: "Weena provides the story's emotional centre, showing that gentleness survives even in a degraded world. Her loss makes the Time Traveller's escape bitter, and the flowers she gave him are the only memento of human kindness in the future."
  - name: "The Eloi"
    role: "The surface-dwelling race of the future"
    summary: "Small, beautiful, childlike people who live in sunlit palaces and eat fruit. They are gentle, playful and lack curiosity, energy or intelligence, and they fear the dark. They are the Morlocks' prey, cared for like cattle."
    analysis: "The Eloi are the descendants of the idle leisured class, and their decline shows what comfort and security might do to humanity. Wells uses them to warn that the end of struggle might end the mind."
  - name: "The Morlocks"
    role: "The underground race of the future"
    summary: "Pale, ape-like creatures with large eyes who live in tunnels and work the machines that keep the Eloi's world running. They emerge at night, take the Eloi, and steal the Time Machine, which they carry into the Sphinx. The Time Traveller repels them with matches and fire."
    analysis: "The Morlocks are the descendants of the working class, a reversal of the class order in which the labouring many now feed on the idle few. They embody Wells's critique of industrial capitalism and his fear of a divided society."
  - name: "The Psychologist"
    role: "A guest; one of the Time Traveller's friends"
    summary: "A sceptical guest who operates the lever on the model Time Machine and watches it vanish. He suspects trickery and questions how the model can have gone into the past or future without being seen."
    analysis: "He voices the doubts of the educated, rational listener and gives the Time Traveller a chance to explain his theory. His presence also shows the group as a set of Victorian professions, each judging the claim from his own field."
  - name: "Filby and the Medical Man"
    role: "Guests at the dinners; sceptical friends"
    summary: "Filby, argumentative and sharp-tongued, and the Medical Man, who studies the Time Traveller's dishevelled state, are among the friends who hear the story. They ask questions and trade jokes, doubting the account and weighing it against his injuries and the strange flowers."
    analysis: "They show how a Victorian audience hears an unbelievable story, sceptical and curious in turn. Their reactions help build the frame that makes the tale seem plausible even as it grows more fantastic."
