    content: "Virgil announces the creature who has been summoned: behold the monster with the pointed tail, who passes mountains and can entrance make through arms and walls, who makes the whole world ail! He beckons it to land by the broken marble pathways. In Greek myth, Geryon was a triple-bodied giant, king of the island of Erytheia, whose cattle were stolen by Heracles as one of his labours. Dante keeps the name and reinvents the monster entirely. The face is a human righteousness, so benignant to the outward view, but the body is a serpent's, the arms hairy to the armpits, the back and chest patterned with knots and rounded shields in a weave finer than any Turk or Tartar loom or the web of Arachne. The tail is a scorpion's, with a poisoned fork that quivers in the air. The design is the portrait of a swindler: a face you would trust on a body you should not touch. The fraudulent are sorted below this creature, and Dante emphasises that his gentle look is a mask. Even the posture is revealing. Geryon rests his front on the stone edge but keeps his tail hidden in the void, like a trader showing the goods and hiding the sting. The monster is also the means of transport, carrying the travellers to the next level."

    content: "Dante watches Geryon settle half on the bank and half in the air, and finds two comparisons. He lies like barges partly in water, partly on dry land; and as afar in gluttonous Germany, watching their prey, alert the beavers stand. Both similes carry an unexpected load. The Germany of the thirteenth century had a reputation among Italians for heavy eating and drinking, and the adjective was a standard stereotype. The beaver, in medieval bestiaries, was said to fish by dangling its tail in the water to lure fish, and Dante's picture of beavers poised on the bank with their tails in the stream is that story. The effect on the reader is a double image: the monster crouches on the stone with its stinger in the void, like a beaver fishing from the shore. That comparison is also a sly one. Beavers, in the bestiary tradition, were hunted for their medicinal glands and were supposed to bite them off to escape, so they carried a moral about self-sacrifice. Dante may not intend all of that, but his taste for animal lore is clear. Even at the edge of a pit, his imagination is drawn to Germany, to river banks and to what the learned believed about beasts."

    content: "While Virgil bargains with Geryon, Dante walks alone to the outermost rim of the seventh circle and finds a woeful folk who beat their hands against the fire and ground like a dog plagued by flies. He cannot recognise a single face. Instead, a purse hangs from the throat of every wight, each with its emblem and its special hue; and every eye seemed feasting on the sight. On one yellow purse Dante sees what seemed a lion's face and mien in blue; on another, a blood-red one, a goose whiter than butter. These are coats of arms, and each belongs to a known Florentine family. The blue lion on yellow was the emblem of the Gianfigliazzi, the white goose on red that of the Ubriachi. Dante gives no name, only the heraldry, because the usurers have been reduced to their wealth. Their identity is the money-bag they cannot take off. The effect is a bleak joke on the aristocratic ideal. They have bought their arms with loans, not with deeds, and they stare at the pouches, hoping for profit, as the fire rains down. The punishment is a perfect economy of shame: the thing they loved is the thing that identifies them, and it is also the thing that drags them down."

The sow was the emblem of the Scrovegni of Padua, and the speaker is generally identified as Reginaldo degli Scrovegni, a banker and moneylender who died around 1290. His son Enrico, wanting to atone for his father's sin, commissioned the chapel in Padua known as the Arena Chapel, and had it frescoed by Giotto between 1303 and 1305. The cycle of paintings is one of the supreme works of European art, and it was probably the setting of a visit from Dante. He did not need the context to make the point. The man sneers at the Florentine newcomer with his tongue thrust out like an ox licking its nose, and calls for his fellow banker, Giovanni Buiamonte, the pink of chivalry whose arms show three beaks. The mocking nods across the pit are the last human exchanges before the descent. Hell's moneylenders speak in the language of their trade: they name each other by their sign."
  - id: "quartan-ague-fear"

    title: "The Fever Dante Compared His Fear To"
    wikipedia_url: "https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quartan_fever"
    image_url: ""
    image_caption: ""
    content: "Virgil is already astride Geryon and tells Dante to climb on in front, so that the tail may not harm him. Dante describes his reaction with a medical simile: like one so close upon the shivering fit of quartan ague that his nails grow blue, and seeing shade he trembles every whit. A quartan ague was a form of malarial fever whose attacks recurred every fourth day, counting inclusively, so that sufferers knew when to dread the next. The first sign was cold, with blue fingernails and shaking, and a sufferer would shiver at the mere sight of shade, since resting in the shade preceded the fit. Dante's readers, living in a country where marsh fevers were common, would have recognised the symptom at once. The simile is exact, and it is also a joke at the pilgrim's own expense: the man who has stood unflinching before demons is reduced by the prospect of a ride. His shame at the sight of Virgil's calm is the usual cure, as before the face of a brave lord his man grows valorous too. He climbs on, wishes to say embrace me, and cannot form the words. Virgil, who has saved him from so many dangers, simply clasps him in his arms."

    wikipedia_url: "https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phaethon"
    image_url: ""

    content: "Geryon turns his tail where his breast was, stretches it like an eel, and glides into the dark. Dante's terror has to be put in comparison with the greatest falls he knows. More terror Phaëthon could hardly feel what time he let the reins abandoned fall, whence Heaven was fired, as still its tracts reveal; nor wretched Icarus, on finding all his plumage moulting as the wax grew hot, while The wrong road! his father loud did call. Both stories come from Ovid's Metamorphoses. Phaethon, son of the sun god, begged to drive his father's chariot for a day, lost control of the horses and scorched the sky, a catastrophe that medieval writers cited as the origin of the Milky Way. Icarus flew too close to the sun with wings of wax and feathers. Both were youths who overreached their ability and fell. Dante invokes them for a reason. He too is a mortal being carried through a place reserved for others, and he too depends on the wisdom of a guide. But the comparison also reverses the myths. Phaethon and Icarus fell because they were proud. Dante flies because he has been sent, and his terror is the proper response of a creature who knows that he is not strong enough alone."

To describe the landing, Dante uses the language of falconry: as a falcon, which long time doth go upon the wing not finding lure or prey, while Ha! the falconer cries, descending so! comes wearied back whence swift it soared away, wheeling a hundred times upon the road, then from its master far sulks angrily. The scene would have been instantly familiar to the aristocratic readers of his day. A hunting falcon was trained to return to the falconer's lure, a bundle of feathers swung on a cord, and a bird that failed to find game would circle down reluctantly, sulking. The comparison implies something about Geryon, who obeys without enthusiasm and departs the moment he is free, and about Dante, who is carried against his nature. The emperor Frederick II, whose court appears earlier in the Inferno, wrote the standard medieval treatise on the subject, The Art of Hunting with Birds. The image closes the long flight with a homely gesture, and the final line, like arrow from the string was swiftly gone, shows how quickly the monster tires of passengers."

'Behold **the monster with the pointed tail**,
    Who passes mountains and can entrance make
    Through arms and walls! who makes the whole world ail,

  Corrupted by him!' Thus my Leader spake,
    And beckoned him that he should land hard by,
    Where short the pathways built of marble break.

  And that foul image of dishonesty
    Moving approached us with his head and chest,
    But to the bank drew not his tail on high.

  His face a human righteousness expressed,
    'Twas so benignant to the outward view;
    A serpent was he as to all the rest.

  On both his arms hair to the arm-pits grew:
    On back and chest and either flank were knot
    And rounded shield portrayed in various hue;

  No Turk or Tartar weaver ever brought
    To ground or pattern a more varied dye;
    Nor by Arachne was such broidery wrought.

  As sometimes by the shore the barges lie
    Partly in water, partly on dry land;
    And as afar in gluttonous Germany,

  Watching their prey, alert **the beavers stand**;
    So did this worst of brutes his foreparts fling
    Upon the stony rim which hems the sand.

  All of his tail in space was quivering,
    Its poisoned fork erecting in the air,
    Which scorpion-like was armèd with a sting.

  My Leader said: 'Now we aside must fare
    A little distance, so shall we attain
    Unto the beast malignant crouching there.'

  So we stepped down upon the right, and then
    A half score steps to the outer edge did pace,
    Thus clearing well the sand and fiery rain.

  And when we were hard by him I could trace
    Upon the sand a little further on
    Some people sitting near to the abyss.

  'That what this belt containeth may be known
    Completely by thee,' then the Master said;
    'To see their case do thou advance alone.

  Let thy inquiries be succinctly made.
    While thou art absent I will ask of him,
    With his strong shoulders to afford us aid.'

  Then, all alone, I on the outmost rim
    Of that Seventh Circle still advancing trod,
    Where sat a woful folk. Full to the brim

  Their eyes with anguish were, and overflowed;
    Their hands moved here and there to win some ease,
    Now from the flames, now from the soil which glowed.

  No otherwise in summer-time one sees,
    Working its muzzle and its paws, the hound
    When bit by gnats or plagued with flies or fleas.

  And I, on scanning some who sat around
    Of those on whom the dolorous flames alight,
    Could recognise not one. I only found

  A purse hung from the throat of every wight,
    Each with its emblem and its special hue;
    And every eye seemed feasting on the sight.

  As I, beholding them, among them drew,
    I saw what seemed **a lion's face and mien**
    Upon a yellow purse designed in blue.

  Still moving on mine eyes athwart the scene
    I saw another scrip, blood-red, display
    A goose more white than butter could have been.

  And one, on whose white wallet blazoned lay
    **A pregnant sow in azure**, to me said:
    'What dost thou in this pit? Do thou straightway

  Begone; and, seeing thou art not yet dead,
    Know that Vitalian, neighbour once of mine,
    Shall on my left flank one day find his bed.

  A Paduan I: all these are Florentine;
    And oft they stun me, bellowing in my ear:
    "Come, Pink of Chivalry, for whom we pine,

  Whose is the purse on which three beaks appear:"'
    Then he from mouth awry his tongue thrust out
    Like ox that licks its nose; and I, in fear

  Lest more delay should stir in him some doubt
    Who gave command I should not linger long,
    Me from those wearied spirits turned about.

  I found my Guide, who had already sprung
    Upon the back of that fierce animal:
    He said to me: 'Now be thou brave and strong.

  By stairs like this we henceforth down must fall.
    Mount thou in front, for I between would sit
    So thee the tail shall harm not nor appal.'

  Like one so close upon the shivering fit
    Of **quartan ague** that his nails grow blue,
    And seeing shade he trembles every whit,

  I at the hearing of that order grew;
    But his threats shamed me, as before the face
    Of a brave lord his man grows valorous too.

  On the great shoulders then I took my place,
    And wished to say, but could not move my tongue
    As I expected: 'Do thou me embrace!'

  But he, who other times had helped me 'mong
    My other perils, when ascent I made
    Sustained me, and strong arms around me flung,

  And, 'Geryon, set thee now in motion!' said;
    'Wheel widely; let thy downward flight be slow;
    Think of the novel burden on thee laid.'

  As from the shore a boat begins to go
    Backward at first, so now he backward pressed,
    And when he found that all was clear below,

  He turned his tail where earlier was his breast;
    And, stretching it, he moved it like an eel,
    While with his paws he drew air toward his chest.

  More terror **Phaëthon** could hardly feel
    What time he let the reins abandoned fall,
    Whence Heaven was fired, as still its tracts reveal;

  Nor wretched Icarus, on finding all
    His plumage moulting as the wax grew hot,
    While, 'The wrong road!' his father loud did call;

  Than what I felt on finding I was brought
    Where nothing was but air and emptiness;
    For save the brute I could distinguish nought.

  He slowly, slowly swims; to the abyss
    Wheeling he makes descent, as I surmise
    From wind felt 'neath my feet and in my face.

  Already on the right I heard arise
    From out the caldron a terrific roar,
    Whereon I stretch my head with down-turned eyes.

  Terror of falling now oppressed me sore;
    Hearing laments, and seeing fires that burned,
    My thighs I tightened, trembling more and more.

  Earlier I had not by the eye discerned
    That we swept downward; scenes of torment now
    Seemed drawing nearer wheresoe'er we turned.

  And **as a falcon** (which long time doth go
    Upon the wing, not finding lure or prey),
    While 'Ha!' the falconer cries, 'descending so!'

  Comes wearied back whence swift it soared away;
    Wheeling a hundred times upon the road,
    Then, from its master far, sulks angrily:

  So we, by Geryon in the deep bestowed,
    Were 'neath the sheer-hewn precipice set down:
    He, suddenly delivered from our load,

  Like arrow from the string was swiftly gone.
