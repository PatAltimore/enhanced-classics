    wikipedia_url: "https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virgil"
    image_url: ""
    image_caption: ""
    content: "Virgil has just come back from the gate, pale and listening. Dante sees his own cowardice painted on his guide's face, and the guide, trying to conceal it, speaks: yet surely we must vanquish in the fight, unless — but with such proffered aid — O how I weary till he come in sight! The sentence veers in mid-air. Virgil starts with a threat, breaks off, and covers his opening words with those behind, which contradicted what at first he said. Dante, who notes the sleight of hand, draws the worst conclusion: perhaps the first words were closer to the truth. What Virgil is concealing is not a plan but a dependence. Someone has promised help and has not yet arrived, and the great Roman, who has guided Dante through six circles with serene authority, has nothing but patience. The scene makes a point that will recur: reason can lead a pilgrim to the walls of the lower city, but its power stops there. Virgil's anxiety is also a lesson in how medieval courage worked. It was not pretending to feel no fear but speaking confidently while fearing, and Dante's attention to the cracks shows how closely the pupil has learned to read the master. The word he uses for the wait, weary, is the plainest in the speech."

    content: "Waiting for help that has not arrived, Virgil tries to reassure Dante that he knows the way: he once descended, he says, beneath a spell of dire Erichtho's laid, who could the corpse with soul inform anew. Erichtho is a witch from Lucan's Pharsalia, the Roman epic of the civil war between Caesar and Pompey, in which she is the most terrifying necromancer in classical literature, a hag who haunts battlefields, steals corpses and revives a dead soldier to prophesy Pompey's defeat. Dante had read Lucan, who is one of the four poets he meets in Limbo, and his borrowing is bold. Virgil claims that Erichtho once forced his ghost back into his body so that she could send him to the very bottom of Hell to fetch a shade from the circle of Judas. No such story exists in any ancient source; Dante invented it. The story is half comfort and half confession. It tells Dante that his guide knows the road, but it also tells us that a witch once commanded the great Roman poet, a humiliating little secret that Virgil mentions with no embarrassment at all. The errand's target is the Giudecca, the last zone of Hell, named for Judas and furthest from the heaven which moves the skies, so Virgil has been to the very bottom. The poet now leading Dante through Christian Hell had to be summoned to it first by a pagan spell."

    content: "On the lofty tower with flaming crest, three hellish furies appear all with blood defaced, with hydras of brilliant green begirt about their waist, snakes and cerastes for their tresses. Virgil names them: Megæra upon the left, Alecto weeping on the right, and Tisiphone between. In Greek myth these were the Erinyes, the avengers of crimes against kin, who served Persephone, queen of the dead. They scream for Medusa, the Gorgon whose glance turned men to stone, so that they may make him stone. They complain that Theseus escaped them when he ventured down, a reference to the hero who tried to abduct Persephone. Virgil's reaction is instant and physical: turn thy back, close thine eyes, and he covers Dante's eyelids with his own hands. Dante's account of the danger is subtler than the myth. Being turned to stone is a figure for despair, the freezing of the will before sin. If the Gorgon is displayed and thou shouldst look, farewell the upward track: seeing her would end the journey. The scene also teaches a lesson in method. Reason, in the form of Virgil, can protect the pilgrim from the sight of despair, but it cannot open the gate. For that, something else must come."

    title: "The Moment Dante Tells Readers to Look for Hidden Meaning"
    wikipedia_url: "https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allegory"
    image_url: ""
    image_caption: ""
    content: "In the middle of the action, Dante stops and speaks directly to us: O ye with judgment gifted to divine, look closely now, and mark what hidden lore lies 'neath the veil of my mysterious line. It is the poem's strongest explicit invitation to read allegorically. Dante's own method was set out in his Convivio and in a letter attributed to him. A text can be read in four ways: literally, as a story; allegorically, as a lesson; morally, as a guide to conduct; and anagogically, as a pointer to the eternal. The warning appears at precisely the point where the allegory is most obscure. The Furies and the Gorgon are at once creatures of myth, a threat to the pilgrim, and figures of the inner life. Exactly what they stand for has been argued for seven centuries, from remorse and despair to the hardening of the heart, and Dante refuses to say. The address is also a literary flourish: just before the arrival of the heavenly messenger, he lifts the curtain to remind us that this is a constructed work, an artifact, with a reader on the other side. The effect is not to break the spell but to deepen it, because the marvel becomes something we have been asked to decipher."

    content: "Inside Dis, Dante sees a vast plain of sepulchres, and for scale he compares it to two places he knows, or at least knows of: as at Arles where Rhone spreads o'er the plain, or Pola, hard upon Quarnaro sound, the sepulchres uneven make the ground. Arles in Provence had a famous burial ground, the Alyscamps, from the Latin for Elysian Fields, a Roman and later Christian necropolis where rows of stone sarcophagi lined an avenue. Medieval legend said that Christ himself had consecrated it, and that the dead from all over the Rhone valley were floated down the river to be buried there. Pola, now Pula in Croatia, lay by the gulf of Quarnaro and had Roman tombs too. Dante names real sites, which reminds us that the great poem is also a traveller's account. But his version is worse. Many a fire is scattered among the tombs, and they glow so hot that no arts which work in iron require more heat. The graves are open, with their lids lifted, so the cries come out freely. A place that real pilgrims knew as a peaceful field of the dead is transformed into a furnace, and the tomb, a symbol of rest, becomes an oven."

    wikipedia_url: "https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heresy"
    image_url: ""
    image_caption: ""
    content: "Dante asks who lies in the tombs, and Virgil answers: the lords of heresies with followers of all sects, a greater band than thou wouldst think. This is the sixth circle, the first inside the city of Dis, and it is reserved for those who denied official doctrine. Heresy in medieval Europe was not an abstract matter. The thirteenth century had seen the Albigensian Crusade against the Cathars in southern France and the creation of the Inquisition to hunt heretics; Dante's own Florence was a place where heretical teachers had been burned. His own judgement here is both severe and subtle. The heretics are shut in fiery tombs, and the tombs are grouped to lodge them like to like, with more or less of heat according to the sin. A heretic's offence was to build a private enclosure of belief, and the punishment makes the enclosure literal. In the next canto we will meet two people who believed there was no life after death and who are boxed in with the evidence. It is worth noticing that Virgil's phrase is all sects. The poem is less interested in doctrine than in the stubborn pride that turns a mistaken idea into an identity, and that pride is precisely what the sealed coffin shows."

The hue which cowardice on my face did paint
    When I beheld my guide return again,
    Put his new colour quicker 'neath restraint.

  Like one who listens did he fixed remain;
    For far to penetrate the air like night,
    And heavy mist, the eye was bent in vain.

  'Yet surely we must vanquish in the fight;'
    Thus he, 'unless--but with such proffered aid--
    **O how I weary till he come in sight**!'

  Well I remarked how he transition made,
    Covering his opening words with those behind,
    Which contradicted what at first he said.

  Nath'less his speech with terror charged my mind,
    For, haply, to the word which broken fell
    Worse meaning than he purposed, I assigned.

  Down to this bottom of the dismal shell
    Comes ever any from the First Degree,
    Where all their pain is, stripped of hope to dwell?

  To this my question thus responded he:
    'Seldom it haps to any to pursue
    The journey now embarked upon by me.

  Yet I ere this descended, it is true,
    Beneath a spell of dire **Erichtho**'s laid,
    Who could the corpse with soul inform anew.

  Short while my flesh of me was empty made
    When she required me to o'erpass that wall,
    From Judas' circle to abstract a shade.

  That is the deepest, darkest place of all,
    And furthest from the heaven which moves the skies;
    I know the way; fear nought that can befall.

  These fens from which vile exhalations rise
    The doleful city all around invest,
    Which now we reach not save in angry wise.'

  Of more he spake nought in my mind doth rest,
    For, with mine eyes, my every thought had been
    Fixed on the lofty tower with flaming crest,

  Where, in a moment and upright, were seen
    Three hellish furies, all with blood defaced,
    And woman-like in members and in mien.

  Hydras of brilliant green begirt their waist;
    Snakes and cerastes for their tresses grew,
    And these were round their dreadful temples braced.

  That they the drudges were, full well he knew,
    Of her who is the queen of endless woes,
    And said to me: 'The fierce Erynnyes view!

  Herself upon the left Megæra shows;
    That is Alecto weeping on the right;
    Tisiphone's between.' Here made he close.

  Each with her nails her breast tore, and did smite
    Herself with open palms. They screamed in tone
    So fierce, I to the Poet clove for fright.

  '**Medusa**, come, that we may make him stone!'
    All shouted as they downward gazed; 'Alack!
    Theseus escaped us when he ventured down.'

  'Keep thine eyes closed and turn to them thy back,
    For if the Gorgon chance to be displayed
    And thou shouldst look, farewell the upward track!'

  Thus spake the Master, and himself he swayed
    Me round about; nor put he trust in mine
    But his own hands upon mine eyelids laid.

  O ye with judgment gifted to divine
    Look closely now, and mark what hidden lore
    Lies 'neath the **veil of my mysterious line**!

  Across the turbid waters came a roar
    And crash of sound, which big with fear arose:
    Because of it fell trembling either shore.

  The fashion of it was as when there blows
    A blast by cross heats made to rage amain,
    Which smites the forest and without repose

  The shattered branches sweeps in hurricane;
    In clouds of dust, majestic, onward flies,
    Wild beasts and herdsmen driving o'er the plain.

  'Sharpen thy gaze,' he bade--and freed mine eyes--
    'Across the foam-flecked immemorial lake,
    Where sourest vapour most unbroken lies.'

  And as the **frogs before the hostile snake**
    Together of the water get them clear,
    And on the dry ground, huddling, shelter take;

  More than a thousand ruined souls in fear
    Beheld I flee from one who, dry of feet,
    Was by the Stygian ferry drawing near.

  Waving his left hand he the vapour beat
    Swiftly from 'fore his face, nor seemed he spent
    Save with fatigue at having this to meet.

  Well I opined that he from Heaven was sent,
    And to my Master turned. His gesture taught
    I should be dumb and in obeisance bent.

  Ah me, how with disdain appeared he fraught!
    He reached the gate, which, touching with a rod,
    He oped with ease, for it resisted not.

  'People despised and banished far from God,'
    Upon the awful threshold then he spoke,
    'How holds in you such insolence abode?

  Why kick against that will which never broke
    Short of its end, if ever it begin,
    And often for you fiercer torments woke?

  Butting 'gainst fate, what can ye hope to win?
    Your Cerberus, as is to you well known,
    Still bears for this a well-peeled throat and chin.'

  Then by the passage foul he back was gone,
    Nor spake to us, but like a man was he
    By other cares absorbed and driven on

  Than that of those who may around him be.
    And we, confiding in the sacred word,
    Moved toward the town in all security.

  We entered without hindrance, and I, spurred
    By my desire the character to know
    And style of place such strong defences gird,

  Entering, begin mine eyes around to throw,
    And see on every hand a vast champaign,
    The teeming seat of torments and of woe.

  And as at **Arles** where Rhone spreads o'er the plain,
    Or Pola, hard upon Quarnaro sound
    Which bathes the boundaries Italian,

  The sepulchres uneven make the ground;
    So here on every side, but far more dire
    And grievous was the fashion of them found.

  For scattered 'mid the tombs blazed many a fire,
    Because of which these with such fervour burned
    No arts which work in iron more require.

  All of the lids were lifted. I discerned
    By keen laments which from the tombs arose
    That sad and suffering ones were there inurned.

  I said: 'O Master, tell me who are those
    Buried within the tombs, of whom the sighs
    Come to our ears thus eloquent of woes?'

  And he to me: 'The **lords of heresies**
    With followers of all sects, a greater band
    Than thou wouldst think, these sepulchres comprise.

  To lodge them like to like the tombs are planned.
    The sepulchres have more or less of heat.'
    Then passed we, turning to the dexter hand,

  'Tween torments and the lofty parapet.
