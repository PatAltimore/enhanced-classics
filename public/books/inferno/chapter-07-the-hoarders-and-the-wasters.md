    content: "Canto 7 opens with a line that has no agreed meaning: Pape Satan! Pape Satan! Aleppe! It is bellowed by Plutus, the guardian of the fourth circle, in accents rough and hard. Dante invites readers to puzzle. Some commentators take papé as the Latin interjection papae, an exclamation of astonishment, so that Plutus is crying out at the arrival of a living man; others see Satan as an invocation to the prince of Hell; and aleppe has been read as the first letter of the Hebrew alphabet, aleph, meaning chief. A few prefer a political reading involving the city of Aleppo. Virgil's reaction makes the best guide: he doesn't translate. He tells Dante not to be afraid and silences the monster with a name, accursed wolf, hinting at the she-wolf of avarice from Canto 1. Plutus is a deliberate fusion of two ancient figures, Pluto the god of the underworld and Plutus the god of wealth, both of whom Dante puts at the gate of the circle of money. And the end of the scene is a brilliant piece of comic collapse: as sails swell and then drag when the mast snaps, so the monster falls to the ground. The cry that no one can read is a perfect voice for greed, which is also noise."

    content: "Dante pauses before the hoarders and wasters, who run in opposite directions pushing great weights with their chests, to find an image: as at Charybdis waves are forward rolled to break on other billows midway met, the people here a counterdance must hold. Charybdis was the whirlpool in the Strait of Messina, opposite the rock Scylla, and every ancient reader remembered it from the Odyssey and the Aeneid, where sailors had to choose which way to steer between them. The comparison fits the geometry of the sin. The hoarders push their stone one way, the wasters another, and when they meet halfway they crash and shout across the divide: why clutch it so, and why to hold it slack? Then each turns and goes back the way he came to meet his opposite number at the other side of the circle. It is the perfect picture of a sin defined by its opposite, because each side is the other's rebuke. The punishment is exactly proportioned to the folly. Those who prized money, whether for keeping or spending, now push it for ever without keeping or spending any of it. Dante's moral geometry makes the fourth circle one of the most precisely drawn in the poem."

    content: "Dante looks at the hoarders and asks whether those on the left, with shaven crown, were clerics. Virgil confirms it with a fierce precision: each wanting thatch of hair upon his head was once a clerk, or pope, or cardinal, in whom abound the ripest growths of greed. The tonsure, the shaved crown of a clergyman, is turned into a mark of shame, and the offence reaches the top of the hierarchy. This is among Dante's most persistent themes. In the Church of his day, the sale of offices and the hunt for money were everyday scandals, and he considered them a betrayal of the poverty on which the Church was founded. Notice that the claim is carefully limited: the greedy clergy are in this circle for avarice, and the traffickers in holy things have a worse hell below. When Dante hopes to recognise a few faces, Virgil refuses him the satisfaction: their witless life, which made them vile, now mocks, dimming their faces still, all searching eyes. The sin has erased their identity. They lived for goods rather than for themselves, and the end of that is a crowd of anonymous creatures unworthy of a name. Even a poet who named his enemies freely finds that greed leaves nothing to name."

    wikipedia_url: "https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sublunary_sphere"

    content: "Virgil delivers a short, severe lecture on the value of what the hoarders and wasters fight over: not all the gold which is beneath the moon could purchase peace for one of these souls. The phrase is not just poetry. In the medieval picture of the universe, inherited from Aristotle and Ptolemy, the moon marked a border. Above its orbit lay the unchanging heavens, made of a perfect substance; below it was the sublunary world of change, decay and death, where everything including gold could be lost or stolen. To say that all gold under the moon cannot buy peace is to say that all that matter can offer is of the changeable kind, and that the soul needs something of the permanent kind. The warning comes with an ironic reminder: these goods are at the dispose of Fortune, thrown by her and fought over by mankind in fierce strife. Even the unit of measurement is a clue. The greatest sum of money that can be imagined is placed against the smallest of heavenly bodies, and it fails. The final cruelty of the circle is that the people condemned for fighting over gold have nothing left to fight over except the weights they push."

    wikipedia_url: "https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortuna"
    image_url: ""
    image_caption: ""
    content: "Dante asks who Fortune is, and Virgil's reply amounts to an argument about providence. Just as God assigned intelligences to guide each of the heavenly spheres, he says, so he appointed one guide and governor of worldly splendours, to move wealth from people to people, from blood to blood, in spite of human wit. Her decisions are as hidden as a serpent in the grass. This is a striking transformation. For the Romans, Fortuna was a goddess of luck, often blind, often cruel, and the medieval tradition kept her wheel, as in Boethius's Consolation of Philosophy, which made her a symbol of change. Dante does not deny her power. He reclassifies her as an angelic minister, a servant of providence rather than a rival to it. What looks like chance to us is order at a scale we cannot see. That means that people who blame her are making a double mistake: she is not responsible for greed, and she is not even malicious. Virgil says people who lift her upon the cross, who curse her, unjustly shift the blame, because she is blest nor hears what any says. Dante's most radical move is to take the goddess of randomness and make her obedient."

    content: "At the end of the fourth circle the travellers follow a dark stream down a terrible pathway to a marsh which by the name of Styx is known, fed by this gloomy brook, at the base of threatening cliffs hewn out of cold grey stone. In Greek myth Styx was a river of the underworld, and the gods themselves swore their strongest oaths by its waters. Achilles was famously dipped in it to make him invulnerable. Virgil mentions it as a nine-fold barrier in the Aeneid. Dante shrinks the legendary river to a foul swamp, a marsh that is also a moat, circling the walls of the city of Dis. It fits the sin that is punished there: the wrathful are mud-smeared, naked and anger-clouded, and they fight each other with fists, feet, chest, head and teeth. Look at the contrast with the rest of this canto, which has been a study in reasoned argument. Virgil lectured on Fortune; now the scene is brute violence, with no commentary needed. The marsh is also a frontier. Beyond it begins the lower Hell, which Dante divides by the waterway that separates the sins of appetite from the sins of malice."

    content: "Not all the sinners in Styx are visible. Virgil points out that beneath the water people utter sighs, making bubbles rise to the surface, and he gives their song: we lived in gloom in the sweet air made jocund by the day, nursing within us melancholy fume; in this black mud we now our gloom display. These are the sullen, the souls who were sulky and morose even when the sun was shining. Their sin is acedia, from the Greek word for lack of care, a heaviness of spirit the early desert monks called the noonday demon. By the time of Dante, it was one of the capital sins, close to what we would call sloth, although it was more an inward sadness than laziness. The punishment follows with precision. Those who let their darkness settle inside are now sunk in physical darkness, and what they could not say in life they cannot say in death: they try to sing, but their hymn comes with gurgling throats, which they in speech unbroken fail to say. Few passages in the Inferno are as psychologically sharp. Dante's own first line, in a dark wood, described a similar gloom, and he knows that the sin he places under water is one he might have shared."

    content: "The canto ends with a tower, and the next begins with a startling line: Io dico, seguitando, I say, continuing. It is an odd way to restart a story that has just been told in the same breath, and it prompted one of the oldest anecdotes about the poem. Giovanni Boccaccio, Dante's earliest biographer, claimed that the first seven cantos were written in Florence before the poet's exile in 1302. When his house was ransacked, a friend found the manuscript and showed it to a local poet, who asked that it be sent to Dante, who by then was living in exile. After some years Dante, taking up the pages again, resumed his work with the opening phrase I say, continuing, as if only a moment had passed. Modern scholars doubt the details, noting that the poem's political prophecies could only have been written after the events they describe, but the story survives for a good reason. The seventh canto closes one distinct movement, from the beasts and the dark wood through Limbo to the circles of appetite, and the next opens a different register: the walled city of Dis, with its towers and signal fires, where sins of malice begin. The tower stands at the hinge."

**Pape Satan**! Pape Satan! Aleppe!
    Plutus began in accents rough and hard:
    And that mild Sage, all-knowing, said to me,

  For my encouragement: 'Pay no regard
    Unto thy fear; whatever power he sways
    Thy passage down this cliff shall not be barred.'

  Then turning round to that inflamèd face
    He bade: 'Accursed wolf, at peace remain;
    And, pent within thee, let thy fury blaze.

  Down to the pit we journey not in vain:
    So rule they where by Michael in Heaven's height
    On the adulterous pride was vengeance ta'en.'

  Then as the bellied sails, by wind swelled tight,
    Suddenly drag whenever snaps the mast;
    Such, falling to the ground, the monster's plight.

  To the Fourth Cavern so we downward passed,
    Winning new reaches of the doleful shore
    Where all the vileness of the world is cast.

  Justice of God! which pilest more and more
    Pain as I saw, and travail manifold!
    Why will we sin, to be thus wasted sore?

  As at **Charybdis** waves are forward rolled
    To break on other billows midway met,
    The people here a counterdance must hold.

  A greater crowd than I had seen as yet,
    With piercing yells advanced on either track,
    Rolling great stones to which their chests were set.

  They crashed together, and then each turned back
    Upon the way he came, while shouts arise,
    'Why clutch it so?' and 'Why to hold it slack?'

  In the dark circle wheeled they on this wise
    From either hand to the opposing part,
    Where evermore they raised insulting cries.

  Thither arrived, each, turning, made fresh start
    Through the half circle a new joust to run;
    And I, stung almost to the very heart,

  Said, 'O my Master, wilt thou make it known
    Who the folk are? Were these all clerks who go
    Before us on the left, with shaven crown?'

  And he replied: 'All of them squinted so
    In mental vision while in life they were,
    They nothing spent by rule. And this they show,

  And with their yelping voices make appear
    When half-way round the circle they have sped,
    And sins opposing them asunder tear.

  Each wanting thatch of hair upon his head
    Was once a **clerk, or pope, or cardinal**,
    In whom abound the ripest growths of greed.'

  And I: 'O Master, surely among all
    Of these I ought some few to recognise,
    Who by such filthy sins were held in thrall.'

  And he to me: 'Vain thoughts within thee rise;
    Their witless life, which made them vile, now mocks--
    Dimming their faces still--all searching eyes.

  Eternally they meet with hostile shocks;
    These rising from the tomb at last shall stand
    With tight clenched fists, and those with ruined locks.

  Squandering or hoarding, they the happy land
    Have lost, and now are marshalled for this fray;
    Which to describe doth no fine words demand.

  Know hence, my Son, how fleeting is the play
    Of goods at the dispose of Fortune thrown,
    And which mankind to such fierce strife betray.

  Not all the **gold which is beneath the moon**
    Could purchase peace, nor all that ever was,
    To but one soul of these by toil undone.'

  'Master,' I said, 'tell thou, ere making pause,
    Who Fortune is of whom thou speak'st askance,
    Who holds all worldly riches in her claws.'

  'O foolish creatures, lost in ignorance!'
    He answer made. 'Now see that the reply
    Thou store, which I concerning her advance.

  He who in knowledge is exalted high,
    Framing all Heavens gave such as should them guide,
    That so each part might shine to all; whereby

  Is equal light diffused on every side:
    And likewise to **one guide and governor**,
    Of worldly splendours did control confide,

  That she in turns should different peoples dower
    With this vain good; from blood should make it pass
    To blood, in spite of human wit. Hence, power,

  Some races failing, other some amass,
    According to her absolute decree
    Which hidden lurks, like serpent in the grass.

  Vain 'gainst her foresight yours must ever be.
    She makes provision, judges, holds her reign,
    As doth his power supreme each deity.

  Her permutations can no truce sustain;
    Necessity compels her to be swift,
    So swift they follow who their turn must gain.

  And this is she whom they so often lift
    Upon the cross, who ought to yield her praise;
    And blame on her and scorn unjustly shift.

  But she is blest nor hears what any says,
    With other primal creatures turns her sphere,
    Jocund and glad, rejoicing in her ways.

  To greater woe now let us downward steer.
    The stars which rose when I began to guide
    Are falling now, nor may we linger here.'

  We crossed the circle to the other side,
    Arriving where a boiling fountain fell
    Into a brooklet by its streams supplied.

  In depth of hue the flood did perse excel,
    And we, with this dim stream to lead us on,
    Descended by a pathway terrible.

  A marsh which by the name of **Styx** is known,
    Fed by this gloomy brook, lies at the base
    Of threatening cliffs hewn out of cold grey stone.

  And I, intent on study of the place,
    Saw people in that ditch, mud-smeared. In it
    All naked stood with anger-clouded face.

  Nor with their fists alone each fiercely hit
    The other, but with feet and chest and head,
    And with their teeth to shreds each other bit.

  'Son, now behold,' the worthy Master said,
    'The souls of those whom anger made a prize;
    And, further, I would have thee certified

  That 'neath the water people utter sighs,
    And make the bubbles to the surface come;
    As thou mayst see by casting round thine eyes.

  Fixed in the mud they say: "We lived in gloom
    In the sweet air made jocund by the day,
    Nursing within us **melancholy fume**.

  In this black mud we now our gloom display."
    This hymn with gurgling throats they strive to sound,
    Which they in speech unbroken fail to say.'

  And thus about the loathsome pool we wound
    For a wide arc, between the dry and soft,
    With eyes on those who gulp the filth, turned round.

  At last we reached **a tower that soared aloft**.
