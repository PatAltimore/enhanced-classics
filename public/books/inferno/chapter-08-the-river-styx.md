    wikipedia_url: "https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virgil"
    image_url: ""
    image_caption: ""
    content: "Dante, seeing one flame answered by another from a distant tower, turns to the Sea of Knowledge, as this translation renders it, and asks what the lights mean. It is a startling title for Virgil: he is the sea itself, not a drop from it, a man of such learning that every question flows into him. Dante's own phrase calls him the sea of all wisdom. It is partly flattery, of course, but it is also a plain fact about the moment. For seven cantos, the pilgrim has leaned on Virgil for explanations: what the beasts mean, who the shades are, why the souls cross the river. He is about to discover how limited that wisdom is. Virgil's reply is practical, not mystical. If the pilgrim could see through the fen-fog, he would already see what approaches, for the answer is a boat. The beacons are a military signal, a common sight on medieval hill-towers: one flame announces how many passengers are coming, a second replies from the far side. Hell, in other words, is a garrison that uses watch fires. And the Sea of Knowledge cannot clear the fog, which is a hint: some obstacles in this poem are not intellectual, and wisdom alone will not open them."

O Phlegyas, Phlegyas, he says, this loud halloo is idle for once, for thou hast us only till the mud we're through. In myth Phlegyas was the king of the Lapiths, a warlike people of Thessaly. When Apollo seduced his daughter Coronis, Phlegyas set fire to the god's temple at Delphi and was killed for it, condemned in the underworld to sit beneath a rock that never falls. In the sixth book of the Aeneid, Virgil gives him a different punishment: he cries out to everyone, learn justice, and do not despise the gods. Dante, who knew the Aeneid thoroughly, changes the job. Phlegyas becomes the ferryman of the wrathful, which is exactly right, because rage was his sin. And a crowning detail follows: as he hears Virgil, he is like one cheated inly, smarting with pain, and his gathering ire he could scarce contain. The boatman of the angry, caught in a trap, simply shows more anger. He is the one figure in Styx who cannot get out of his own sin."

    wikipedia_url: "https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gospel_of_Luke"
    image_url: ""

    content: "A muddy soul rises in front of the boat and seizes its side, and Dante answers him in anger. Virgil heaves the sinner off with the words among the other dogs be gone, then throws his arms around Dante's neck and kisses his face. Soul disdainful, he says, blessed she in whom thou wast conceived. The line is an echo of the Gospel of Luke, where a woman in the crowd cries out to Jesus: blessed is the womb that bore thee. By lifting those words and turning them on a pupil who has just shown righteous contempt, Virgil is saying something unexpected. He is praising Dante for exactly the opposite of pity. In the fifth canto Dante fainted from compassion; here he is rewarded for hardness. The reason for the change is that this sinner is not a victim. He is the type who in the world great haughtiness displayed, and whose memory has no deeds to adorn it. Many a king, Virgil adds, shall wallow here like swine in mire. The kiss is the strongest sign of approval in the whole canto, and it carries a sharp message: Dante's heart is being retrained, one reaction at a time, to side with justice."

    content: "At Dante's request, Virgil promises to let him see the muddy sinner soused in the tide, and moments later the crowd falls on him roaring, Philip Argenti! at him! The victim, a furious spirit Florentine, turns his teeth upon himself and tears. Dante adds that he praises and blesses God for it even yet, one of the most shocking lines in the poem. The target was a real man. Filippo Argenti degli Adimari belonged to a powerful Black Guelph family, and Boccaccio relates that his nickname was a joke on his wealth, since he reportedly had his horse shod with silver. The family would later seize the property of Dante and his exiled friends. Boccaccio also tells a story of a quarrel in which Argenti gave Dante a slap, though it is hard to confirm. For centuries commentators have worried about Dante's mood. How can a poem so full of compassion exult in a man's torment? The best answer is structural. Dante's own pride is a theme, and Virgil's praise in the previous lines suggests that he is rewarded here for rejecting a proud man's help. The poem lets itself be small, once, so that the larger lesson of mercy and justice will not be abstract."

    title: "Why the City of Dis Is Guarded by Fallen Angels"
    wikipedia_url: "https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dis_(Dante)"
    image_url: ""
    image_caption: ""
    content: "Phlegyas drops his passengers at the iron walls of a city with a name borrowed from classical myth: Dis, one of the Roman titles of the god of the underworld. Dante had earlier glimpsed its towers as mosques, vermilion as if fresh from furnace, the nearest he could come to an architecture of fire. Virgil explains that everlasting fires dwell within them, whence they appear glowing hot. On the walls stand more than a thousand, who from Heaven were cast, demanding to know who dares walk among the dead before his death. Their origin is the point. They are the rebel angels who fell with Lucifer, and the passage marks a shift of ranks. Until now Dante's guardians have been classical monsters: Charon, Minos, Cerberus, Plutus, Phlegyas. From here, the opposition is Christian and fully intelligent, a demonic army defending a fortified city against the Church's own pilgrim. The change of tone is deliberate. Sins of weakness, which are the sins of the upper circles, are handled by creatures of myth; sins of malice, which require a will turned against God, belong to the city of the devil. The demons' sneering offer, come thou, but let that one be gone, leaves Dante with no one to trust."
  - id: "sweet-father-virgil-abandons"
    trigger: "sweet Father"
    title: "The Moment Virgil Becomes a Father in Dante's Voice"
    wikipedia_url: "https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virgil"
    image_url: ""
    image_caption: ""
    content: "When the demons ask Virgil to come in alone, Dante panics. Beloved Guide, he begs, who more than seven times set me in security, leave me not thus undone. Virgil tells him to wait, and goes. Then comes a line of unusual feeling: thus goes he, thus am I abandoned by my sweet Father. It is the first time Dante has called Virgil father. In the earlier cantos he was the poet's master, lord and guide, titles of authority; now, when the protection is withdrawn, the language changes to the language of a child. The ordinary reader may overlook the shift, but a careful one will hear a rehearsal of the poem's ending. When Virgil vanishes at the summit of Purgatory, Dante will turn to him like a child running to his mother, and call him dolcissimo patre, sweetest father. Here the abandonment is brief, and Virgil returns, but the emotional pattern is set. The pilgrim, left to hover between yes and no, with contrary thoughts contending in his head, learns what it is to lack his support. The seven earlier rescues Dante lists are a quiet roll of Virgil's service, and the canto shows how thoroughly the pilgrim has come to depend on it."

    wikipedia_url: "https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harrowing_of_Hell"
    image_url: ""
    image_caption: ""
    content: "Virgil returns downcast after the gates slam in his face, but he reassures Dante with a reminder of history: this arrogance of theirs is nothing new; they showed it once at a less secret door, which stands unbolted since. The door he means is the gate with the dark inscription that Dante read in Canto 3. The demons tried to bar it against Christ when he descended to Limbo after the Crucifixion to free the Old Testament righteous, the event Virgil described in Canto 4 as the arrival of a Potentate. They failed, and the gate has stood open ever since, which is why Dante and Virgil walked in without hindrance. The comparison does a lot of work. It says that the demons have lost this fight before, that the resistance is theatrical, and that someone is already on his way. Thence, even now, is one who hastens down through all the circles, guideless, to this shore, and he shall win us entrance to the town. The messenger is not named, but the clue is here: he will come without a guide because he needs none. Hell's front gate was broken by Christ; its second will be opened by his emissary, and the sequence builds a hidden pattern of rescue across the first nine cantos."

I say, continuing, that long before
    To its foundations we approachèd nigh
    Our eyes went travelling to the top of the tower;

  For, hung out there, two flames we could espy.
    Then at such distance, scarce our eyesight made
    It clearly out, another gave reply.

  And, to the **Sea of Knowledge** turned, I said:
    'What meaneth this? and what reply would yield
    That other light, and who have it displayed?'

  'Thou shouldst upon the impure watery field,'
    He said, 'already what approaches know,
    But that the fen-fog holds it still concealed.'

  Never was arrow yet from sharp-drawn bow
    Urged through the air upon a swifter flight
    Than what I saw a tiny vessel show,

  Across the water shooting into sight;
    A single pilot served it for a crew,
    Who shouted: 'Art thou come, thou guilty sprite?'

  'O **Phlegyas**, Phlegyas, this thy loud halloo!
    For once,' my Lord said, 'idle is and vain.
    Thou hast us only till the mud we're through.'

  And, as one cheated inly smarts with pain
    When the deceit wrought on him is betrayed,
    His gathering ire could Phlegyas scarce contain.

  Into the bark my Leader stepped, and made
    Me take my place beside him; nor a jot,
    Till I had entered, was it downward weighed.

  Soon as my Guide and I were in the boat,
    To cleave the flood began the ancient prow,
    Deeper than 'tis with others wont to float.

  Then, as the stagnant ditch we glided through,
    One smeared with filth in front of me arose
    And said: 'Thus coming ere thy period, who

  Art thou?' And I: 'As one who forthwith goes
    I come; but thou defiled, how name they thee?'
    'I am but one who weeps,' he said. 'With woes,'

  I answered him, 'with tears and misery,
    Accursèd soul, remain; for thou art known
    Unto me now, all filthy though thou be.'

  Then both his hands were on the vessel thrown;
    But him my wary Master backward heaved,
    Saying: 'Do thou 'mong the other dogs be gone!'

  Then to my neck with both his arms he cleaved,
    And kissed my face, and, 'Soul disdainful,' said,
    'O **blessed she in whom thou wast conceived**!

  He in the world great haughtiness displayed.
    No deeds of worth his memory adorn;
    And therefore rages here his sinful shade.

  And many are there by whom crowns are worn
    On earth, shall wallow here like swine in mire,
    Leaving behind them names o'erwhelmed in scorn.'

  And I: 'O Master, I have great desire
    To see him well soused in this filthy tide,
    Ere from the lake we finally retire.'

  And he: 'Or ever shall have been descried
    The shore by thee, thy longing shall be met;
    For such a wish were justly gratified.'

  A little after in such fierce onset
    The miry people down upon him bore,
    I praise and bless God for it even yet.

  '**Philip Argenti**! at him!' was the roar;
    And then that furious spirit Florentine
    Turned with his teeth upon himself and tore.

  Here was he left, nor wins more words of mine.
    Now in my ears a lamentation rung,
    Whence I to search what lies ahead begin.

  And the good Master told me: 'Son, ere long
    We to the city called of Dis draw near,
    Where in great armies cruel burghers throng.'

  And I: 'Already, Master, I appear
    Mosques in the valley to distinguish well,
    Vermilion, as if they from furnace were

  Fresh come.' And he: 'Fires everlasting dwell
    Within them, whence appear they glowing hot,
    As thou discernest in this lower hell.'

  We to the moat profound at length were brought,
    Which girds that city all disconsolate;
    The walls around it seemed of iron wrought.

  Not without fetching first a compass great,
    We came to where with angry cry at last:
    'Get out,' the boatman yelled; 'behold the gate!'

  More than a thousand, who **from Heaven were cast**,
    I saw above the gates, who furiously
    Demanded: 'Who, ere death on him has passed,

  Holds through the region of the dead his way?'
    And my wise Master made to them a sign
    That he had something secretly to say.

  Then ceased they somewhat from their great disdain,
    And said: 'Come thou, but let that one be gone
    Who thus presumptuous enters on this reign.

  Let him retrace his madcap way alone,
    If he but can; thou meanwhile lingering here,
    Through such dark regions who hast led him down.'

  Judge, reader, if I was not filled with fear,
    Hearing the words of this accursèd threat;
    For of return my hopes extinguished were.

  'Beloved Guide, who more than seven times set
    Me in security, and safely brought
    Through frightful dangers in my progress met,

  Leave me not thus undone;' I him besought:
    'If further progress be to us denied,
    Let us retreat together, tarrying not.'

  The Lord who led me thither then replied:
    'Fear not: by One so great has been assigned
    Our passage, vainly were all hindrance tried.

  Await me here, and let thy fainting mind
    Be comforted and with good hope be fed,
    Not to be left in this low world behind.'

  Thus goes he, thus am I abandonèd
    By my **sweet Father**. I in doubt remain,
    With Yes and No contending in my head.

  I could not hear what speech he did maintain,
    But no long time conferred he in that place,
    Till, to be first, all inward raced again.

  And then the gates were closed in my Lord's face
    By these our enemies; outside stood he;
    Then backward turned to me with lingering pace,

  With downcast eyes, and all the bravery
    Stripped from his brows; and he exclaimed with sighs;
    'Who dare deny the doleful seats to me!'

  And then he said: 'Although my wrath arise,
    Fear not, for I to victory will pursue,
    Howe'er within they plot, the enterprise.

  This arrogance of theirs is nothing new;
    They showed it once at a **less secret door**
    Which stands unbolted since. Thou didst it view,

  And saw the dark-writ legend which it bore.
    Thence, even now, is one who hastens down
    Through all the circles, guideless, to this shore,

  And he shall win us entrance to the town.'
