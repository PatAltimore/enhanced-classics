    title: "Dante's Famous Mix-Up About a Pope, a Deacon and a Heresy"
    wikipedia_url: "https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Anastasius_II"
    image_url: ""
    image_caption: ""
    content: "To shelter from the stench of lower Hell, the poets duck behind a great tomb whose inscription reads: Pope Anastasius is within me thrust, whom the straight way Photinus made to miss. The inscription is a puzzle. Anastasius II was pope from 496 to 498 and was accused, in a medieval chronicle tradition, of having tried to bring Acacius, a patriarch of Constantinople, back into communion with Rome. Acacius was linked in turn to a deacon of Thessalonica named Photinus, who held that Christ was a mere man. Historically the link between Photinus the heretic of the fourth century and Anastasius the pope of the fifth is shaky, and some scholars think Dante confused Pope Anastasius with the Byzantine Emperor Anastasius I, who really did favour heretics. What matters in the poem is the effect. A pope in a burning tomb is a reminder that no rank is protected, and it is the first of many papal sinners the poem will name. The scene also turns a nasty odour into an occasion. While they pause to let their noses grow accustomed to the gust from the abyss, Virgil delivers the lecture on the shape of the rest of Hell. The stink buys time, and the poem uses it to explain its own architecture."
  - id: "fraud-belongs-to-man-alone"
    trigger: "fraud to man alone doth appertain"
    title: "Why Dante Thought Lying Was Worse Than Murder"
    wikipedia_url: "https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Officiis"
    image_url: ""
    image_caption: ""
    content: "Virgil sets out the plan of lower Hell in a few quick tercets. Injury to others, he says, comes either by force or by fraud; since fraud to man alone doth appertain, God hates it most, and hence the fraudulent band, set lowest down, endure a fiercer pain. It is a reasoned argument. Violence is a sin shared with beasts: lions kill and wolves tear. Fraud requires reason, the faculty that distinguishes human beings, and uses it against its purpose. The reasoning echoes Cicero's De Officiis, which says that there are two ways of doing wrong, by force or fraud, the one belonging to the lion and the other to the fox, and that fraud is the more contemptible. Dante's moral scale therefore ranks sins by how fully they corrupt the human mind. The weak, who give in to appetite, sit near the top; the violent, who use will against others, below them; the fraudulent, who use intelligence to deceive, near the bottom; and the worst of all, the traitors, who deceive those who trust them, at the very centre. The effect is that the lowest part of Hell is crowded with clever people. The poem's final judgement is that intelligence is a gift whose misuse is the gravest crime."
  - id: "violence-against-self-gamblers"
    trigger: "gamble"
    title: "The Surprising Company Suicides Keep in Dante's Hell"
    wikipedia_url: "https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suicide"
    image_url: ""
    image_caption: ""
    content: "Virgil sorts violence into three kinds: against neighbours, against oneself, and against God. The middle group is the most unusual. In the next ring are they who on themselves loss of your world entail, who gamble and their substance madly spend, and who, when called to joy, lament and wail. Suicides are expected; the other two are not. Dante defines violence against oneself as including violence against one's own goods, so the spendthrift, who throws his property to the winds, ranks with the man who takes his own life. In the thirteenth canto we will meet both groups: the suicides turned into thorny trees, and the squanderers hunted through the wood by black dogs. The most puzzling category is the third, those who weep when called to joy. This is probably the gloomy temper that wastes its own happiness through despair. The classification shows how Dante's theology defined harm. A life is not only one's own to destroy but also a gift, and a person who treats the gift carelessly, whether by gambling away a fortune or by refusing to be glad, is attacking a goodness that was lent. It is a harsh view, and it explains why the wasters in the fourth circle were pursued and not simply ignored."
  - id: "sodom-and-cahors-usury"
    trigger: "Sodom and Cahors"
    title: "Why a French Town Is Named Beside Sodom in Hell"
    wikipedia_url: "https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cahors"
    image_url: ""
    image_caption: ""
    content: "Violence against God, Virgil continues, includes denial and blasphemy, and those who sinned against nature and art: hence Sodom and Cahors are doomed to lie within the narrowest circlet surely sealed. Sodom needs no explanation, but Cahors does. It is a town in southwestern France that, in the thirteenth century, became a byword for moneylending. Its bankers, the Caorsini, were so closely identified with usury that the word cahorsin became a general term for a lender at interest in medieval Europe, and the Church repeatedly condemned them. Dante puts the town beside Sodom to make a point about nature and art. Sodomy defies the nature that God gave, and usury defies the art through which people honestly make a living. Both, in his view, treat what was designed for another end as a source of profit. A modern reader may be struck by the pairing, but it was entirely conventional for Dante's time, and Virgil's words make no apology. The geography is also worth noticing. A town far from Florence, a city of the plain from Genesis, and a bank of moneylenders all appear in the same line, and the effect is to show that the circle holds people from every age."
  - id: "crimson-city-boundary-dis"
    trigger: "crimson city"
    title: "Why the Sins of Weakness Are Left Outside the City"
    wikipedia_url: "https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dis_(Dante)"
    image_url: ""
    image_caption: ""
    content: "Having heard the plan of the lower circles, Dante asks the question that has been bothering him since the walls of Dis: why are the lustful, gluttonous, avaricious and wrathful, the tempest-driven and the rain-beaten, not within the crimson city ta'en for punishment, if hateful unto God? And if they are not hateful, why are they in pain at all? The crimson city is Dis, glowing red from the furnace in its walls. The question is a logical one. Dante has noticed that Hell has a boundary with the Styx and Dis, and that the souls on the near side are kept apart from those within. The answer depends on a distinction between a lack of self-control and a deliberate choice of evil. The upper circles hold people who gave way to appetite; the city holds those who used their will against others or against God. The first group sinned from weakness, the second from malice. The city's architecture therefore marks a moral threshold. Outside the walls are sinners we might meet in everyday life. Inside, we are in a world of deliberate harm. Dante's question is a kind of reader's reflex, and Virgil's reply is the poem's first systematic account of what the layout means."
  - id: "aristotle-ethics-three-moods"
    trigger: "thy Ethics"
    title: "The Philosopher's Triple Scheme Hidden Behind Hell's Map"
    wikipedia_url: "https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicomachean_Ethics"
    image_url: ""
    image_caption: ""
    content: "Virgil answers Dante's question with a gentle rebuke: hast thou the memory of the passage lost in which thy Ethics for their subject treat of the three moods by Heaven abhorred the most? The book is Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics, which medieval scholars read in Latin translation, and the passage is the one in which Aristotle distinguishes three bad conditions of character. Malice is deliberate wrongdoing; bestiality is a brutish extremity of vice; and incontinence is a failure to act on what one knows is right. The scheme gives Dante a framework: incontinence, the weakest, covers the upper circles; violence, equivalent to bestiality, the middle; and malice, in the form of fraud, the deepest. Virgil adds that incontinence offends God less and meets lesser blame. He is also making a pointed claim about learning. Dante, who wrote philosophical treatises of his own, is told that he already has the key in his own notes. The line is a small tribute to Aristotle, whom Dante called the master of those who know, and a reminder of the poem's method. Its architecture was not invented by revelation alone. It is built on a pagan philosopher's account of human nature, which a Christian poet found compatible with the doctrine."
  - id: "art-grandchild-of-god"
    trigger: "As grandchild then to God your art is kin"
    title: "How Human Work Became God's Grandchild in Dante's Argument"
    wikipedia_url: "https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Usury"
    image_url: ""
    image_caption: ""
    content: "Dante still wants to know why usury offends God, and Virgil's answer rests on one of the most beautiful lines in the canto: as grandchild then to God your art is kin. The logic goes like this. Nature follows the divine mind, as Aristotle's Physics shows, and human art, meaning work, craft and skill, imitates nature as far as it can, as a pupil follows a teacher. So art is the child of nature and the grandchild of God. Then Virgil turns to Genesis: from nature and from art, the book teaches, people are meant to gain their living, by tilling the soil and by labour. The usurer, who makes money from money, steps outside both. His hope relies on other ground, and nature and nature's child have his disdain. Medieval economics thought of money as barren, since it does not breed, and a coin lent at interest was a kind of unnatural reproduction. Modern readers rarely accept the argument, but the line about art is memorable on its own. It gives manual work and craft a lineage that runs back to the Creator, and it is one of the rare moments in the Inferno when the poem praises the dignity of ordinary labour."
  - id: "caurus-wain-time-of-night"
    trigger: "Caurus"
    title: "How Dante Tells the Time in a World Without Sunlight"
    wikipedia_url: "https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ursa_Major"
    image_url: ""
    image_caption: ""
    content: "There is no sun in Hell, so Dante keeps time by the stars. Virgil ends the canto with an astronomical tag: the Fishes over the horizon line quiver; o'er Caurus now stands all the Wain; and further yonder does the cliff decline. The Fishes are the constellation Pisces, which at this season rises just before the Sun, and the Wain is Charles's Wain, the Great Bear or Big Dipper, now lying over Caurus, the northwest, in the sky. Together they put the hour at a couple of hours before dawn on Holy Saturday, April 9, 1300. The detail does something for the story. The pilgrim met Virgil at dawn on Good Friday and entered the gate at dusk, so by now most of a night has passed since the gate. Virgil's remark is also a nudge. Each time he mentions time, as he did in the seventh canto when the stars that rose when I began to guide were falling, it is a gentle reminder that the journey has a schedule, and that their way down must keep to it. The poem never loses its sense that Hell is a place with a clock. Dante's habit of timing events by constellations links the lowest pit to the highest heavens."

We at the margin of a lofty steep
    Made of great shattered stones in circle bent,
    Arrived where worser torments crowd the deep.

  So horrible a stench and violent
    Was upward wafted from the vast abyss,
    Behind the cover we for shelter went

  Of a great tomb where I saw written this:
    '**Pope Anastasius** is within me thrust,
    Whom the straight way Photinus made to miss.'

  'Now on our course a while we linger must,'
    The Master said, 'be but our sense resigned
    A little to it, and the filthy gust

  We shall not heed.' Then I: 'Do thou but find
    Some compensation lest our time should run
    Wasted.' And he: 'Behold, 'twas in my mind.

  Girt by the rocks before us, O my son,
    Lie three small circles,' he began to tell,
    'Graded like those with which thou now hast done,

  All of them filled with spirits miserable.
    That sight of them may thee henceforth suffice.
    Hear how and wherefore in these groups they dwell.

  Whate'er in Heaven's abhorred as wickedness
    Has injury for its end; in others' bane
    By fraud resulting or in violent wise.

  Since **fraud to man alone doth appertain**,
    God hates it most; and hence the fraudulent band,
    Set lowest down, endure a fiercer pain.

  Of the violent is the circle next at hand
    To us; and since three ways is violence shown,
    'Tis in three several circuits built and planned.

  To God, ourselves, or neighbours may be done
    Violence, or on the things by them possessed;
    As reasoning clear shall unto thee make known.

  Our neighbour may by violence be distressed
    With grievous wounds, or slain; his goods and lands
    By havoc, fire, and plunder be oppressed.

  Hence those who wound and slay with violent hands,
    Robbers, and spoilers, in the nearest round
    Are all tormented in their various bands.

  Violent against himself may man be found,
    And 'gainst his goods; therefore without avail
    They in the next are in repentance drowned

  Who on themselves loss of your world entail,
    Who **gamble** and their substance madly spend,
    And who when called to joy lament and wail.

  And even to God may violence extend
    By heart denial and by blasphemy,
    Scorning what nature doth in bounty lend.

  **Sodom and Cahors** hence are doomed to lie
    Within the narrowest circlet surely sealed;
    And such as God within their hearts defy.

  Fraud, 'gainst whose bite no conscience findeth shield,
    A man may use with one who in him lays
    Trust, or with those who no such credence yield.

  Beneath this latter kind of it decays
    The bond of love which out of nature grew;
    Hence, in the second circle herd the race

  To feigning given and flattery, who pursue
    Magic, false coining, theft, and simony,
    Pimps, barrators, and suchlike residue.

  The other form of fraud makes nullity
    Of natural bonds; and, what is more than those,
    The special trust whence men on men rely.

  Hence in the place whereon all things repose,
    The narrowest circle and the seat of Dis,
    Each traitor's gulfed in everlasting woes.'

  'Thy explanation, Master, as to this
    Is clear,' I said, 'and thou hast plainly told
    Who are the people stowed in the abyss.

  But tell why those the muddy marshes hold,
    The tempest-driven, those beaten by the rain,
    And such as, meeting, virulently scold,

  Are not within the **crimson city** ta'en
    For punishment, if hateful unto God;
    And, if not hateful, wherefore doomed to pain?'

  And he to me: 'Why wander thus abroad,
    More than is wont, thy wits? or how engrossed
    Is now thy mind, and on what things bestowed?

  Hast thou the memory of the passage lost
    In which **thy Ethics** for their subject treat
    Of the three moods by Heaven abhorred the most--

  Malice and bestiality complete;
    And how, compared with these, incontinence
    Offends God less, and lesser blame doth meet?

  If of this doctrine thou extract the sense,
    And call to memory what people are
    Above, outside, in endless penitence,

  Why from these guilty they are sundered far
    Thou shalt discern, and why on them alight
    The strokes of justice in less angry war.'

  'O Sun that clearest every troubled sight,
    So charmed am I by thy resolving speech,
    Doubt yields me joy no less than knowing right.

  Therefore, I pray, a little backward reach,'
    I asked, 'to where thou say'st that usury
    Sins 'gainst God's bounty; and this mystery teach.'

  He said: 'Who gives ear to Philosophy
    Is taught by her, nor in one place alone,
    What nature in her course is governed by,

  Even Mind Divine, and art which thence hath grown;
    And if thy Physics thou wilt search within,
    Thou'lt find ere many leaves are open thrown,

  This art by yours, far as your art can win,
    Is followed close--the teacher by the taught;
    **As grandchild then to God your art is kin**.

  And from these two--do thou recall to thought
    How Genesis begins--should come supplies
    Of food for man, and other wealth be sought.

  And, since another plan the usurer plies,
    Nature and nature's child have his disdain;
    Because on other ground his hope relies.

  But come, for to advance I now am fain:
    The Fishes over the horizon line
    Quiver; o'er **Caurus** now stands all the Wain;

  And further yonder does the cliff decline.'
