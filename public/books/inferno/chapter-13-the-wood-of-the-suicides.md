    content: "The wood of the suicides has no path, no green, no smooth bough, and for fruit only poisonous thorns. Among the trees nest the loathsome Harpies, who from the Strophades the Trojans chased with dire predictions of a woe to come. Dante is quoting the Aeneid. In the third book Aeneas and his crew land on the Strophades islands, slaughter cattle for a feast and are driven off by the Harpies, filthy bird-women who snatch the food and defile it. One of them, Celaeno, prophesies that the Trojans will not found their city until hunger has forced them to eat their tables. Dante describes them as great winged, with human necks and faces, feathered bellies and clawed feet, and gives them a job: they feed on the leaves of the trees, wounding them so that they bleed and cry out. In Greek myth the Harpies were snatchers of the dead, carrying souls to the underworld, so their presence here is apt, and the geography is not accidental. The wood is located between Corneto and Cecina, a stretch of the Tuscan Maremma, a wild coastal region that Dante's readers knew as untamed. The place is real, the bird-women are ancient, and the combination is designed to make a nightmare look like a map."

    content: "Hearing wails with no visible source, Dante is told by Virgil to snap a twig from one of the trees. He does, and the trunk demands: why dost thou tear me? Black blood wells from the break, and the bush asks a second time whether he has no pity in him. Virgil then explains why he asked Dante to do it, and the explanation is quietly self-aware: had Dante believed what he read in my lay, his hand would never have been thrust at thee. The scene is lifted from the third book of the Aeneid, in which Aeneas pulls a branch from a myrtle on the coast of Thrace and finds that it bleeds: the tree is the grave of Polydorus, Priam's youngest son, murdered for his gold and planted over by spears that took root. Dante intensifies the incident in two ways. He makes the trees souls rather than burial markers, and he stages the revelation as a moral test. Virgil is not scolding Dante for violence; he is showing him that he cannot trust his eyes. The line Though trees we be, once men were all of us makes the connection plain. Dante has hurt a person, and the poem makes him feel it with his own hand."

Pier della Vigna was a poor boy from Capua who rose to be chief minister, judge and secretary to Emperor Frederick II, the architect of the Sicilian legal code and a member of the court circle in which Italian poetry was born. He wrote sonnets and canzoni and is counted among the first Italian poets. The key image has a double echo. Peter, in Matthew's Gospel, receives the keys that open and shut heaven, and Pier is a variant of Peter; the poem has just shown him as a rival to the papal throne in his influence over the emperor's mind. He tells us that he was so loyal that it cost him sleep and haleness of his blood. In 1249 he was arrested for treason, possibly for poisoning or for corruption, and was blinded. He died soon afterwards, probably by suicide, in prison. Dante gives him the first speech of the wood and treats him with sympathy, as a man destroyed by a world he served too well."

    content: "Pier explains what ruined him: the harlot who removeth nevermore from Caesar's house eyes ignorant of shame, a common curse, of courts the special sore. She is Envy, which Dante depicts as a prostitute who lingers at the emperor's palace and sets the courtiers against the man who has risen highest. They in turn set Augustus, the imperial title, against him, until his glad honours bitter woes became. The picture is of a court in which a man's success becomes the evidence against him, and it matches Dante's own experience of Florentine politics, where the sin of envy is named in the sixth canto as one of the three sparks that set every heart on fire. Pier's reaction was to flee his disgrace in death, thinking by means of death disdain to flee, and so against his just self unjustly did conspire. The line rolls the problem into one phrase: a man who was innocent made himself the culprit. He swears by the new roots of this tree that he never broke faith with his lord. The final plea is not for forgiveness but for reputation: let him restore my memory, overthrown and suffering yet because of envy's stroke."

    trigger: "Men justly lose what from themselves they've ta'en"
    title: "Why the Suicides Alone Will Never Get Their Bodies Back"
    wikipedia_url: "https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suicide"
    image_url: ""
    image_caption: ""
    content: "Virgil prompts Pier to explain how souls become trees, and he answers in a rush of air. When the fierce soul tears itself from the body, Minos sends it to the seventh depth; there it falls at random into the wood, germinating like seed of spelt that's sown, and grows from sapling young into a tree. The Harpies feed on its leaves, which hurts it and opens loopholes whence its sighs are wrung. Then comes the grim doctrine of the Resurrection. On the Last Day, all other souls shall return to their vestments, their bodies, but the suicides shall ne'er be clad in them: men justly lose what from themselves they've ta'en. Their bodies will be dragged to the wood and hung, each on the thorn of its destructive shade. It is the poem's most vivid image of what the act is: a rejection of the body that the body will outlast. The punishment is also a mirror. Those who threw away their flesh are kept as unmoving wood, unable to move, unable to flee. The doctrine that the suicides alone cannot reclaim the flesh is Dante's own invention, and its cruelty is deliberate. He wanted the horror of the act to be visible in the landscape."

    content: "While the poets linger at the bush, they hear a sudden uproar like a huntsman's boar crashing through the trees. Two naked, scratched figures burst through the barriers, the foremost crying, speed to my rescue, death! The second taunts him: Lano, thy legs bested thee not so at the tournament of Toppo. Lano is Arcolano di Squarcia Maconi of Siena, a young man who squandered his fortune with a group of friends and is said to have died in 1288 fighting in a skirmish near the Pieve al Toppo, possibly choosing death rather than flight. The taunt is merciless: the legs that failed him at Toppo now do not run fast enough. Behind the men comes a pack of black, ravenous dogs, like greyhounds from their leashes slipped, which catch the second runner, tear him piecemeal and carry off his limbs. Here is the logic of the spendthrifts. They wasted the goods of the world as if to destroy them, and now they are themselves hunted down and wasted. The suicides in the wood are rooted; the squanderers are the opposite, in perpetual flight. The sin of throwing away one's means is shown as a chase, and the end of any chase is the dogs."

    wikipedia_url: "https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florence"
    image_url: ""

    content: "A final bush, torn by the dogs and weeping, is identified by a Florentine voice that never names itself. It says only: my city for the Baptist changed of yore her former patron; wherefore, in return, he with his art will make her aye deplore. The speaker is a Florentine suicide, speaking of the city's old devotion. In Dante's day it was believed that Florence had originally been dedicated to Mars, the god of war, and that when the citizens converted to Christianity they adopted Saint John the Baptist instead. The old god, offended, was thought to keep Florence in a state of strife. Moreover, a mutilated statue said to depict Mars stood at the end of the Ponte Vecchio, and the bush notes that were it not some image doth remain of him where Arno's crossed, the citizens who rebuilt the town on the ashes left by Attila would have laboured in vain. Dante is here half-quoting local superstition and half-mocking it. The Florentine who hangs himself in his own house is not named, and that anonymity gives him an uncomfortable generality. Dante lets him speak last in the circle because the problem of Florence, civil war and a self-destroying people, is also a problem of suicide."

Ere Nessus landed on the other shore
    We for our part within a forest drew,
    Which of no pathway any traces bore.

  Not green the foliage, but of dusky hue;
    Not smooth the boughs, but gnarled and twisted round;
    For apples, poisonous thorns upon them grew.

  No rougher brakes or matted worse are found
    Where savage beasts betwixt Corneto roam
    And Cecina, abhorring cultured ground.

  The loathsome **Harpies** nestle here at home,
    Who from the Strophades the Trojans chased
    With dire predictions of a woe to come.

  Great winged are they, but human necked and faced,
    With feathered belly, and with claw for toe;
    They shriek upon the bushes wild and waste.

  'Ere passing further, I would have thee know,'
    The worthy Master thus began to say,
    'Thou'rt in the second round, nor hence shalt go

  Till by the horrid sand thy footsteps stay.
    Give then good heed, and things thou'lt recognise
    That of my words will prove the verity.'

  Wailings on every side I heard arise:
    Of who might raise them I distinguished nought;
    Whereon I halted, smitten with surprise.

  I think he thought that haply 'twas my thought
    The voices came from people 'mong the trees,
    Who, to escape us, hiding-places sought;

  Wherefore the Master said: 'From one of these
    Snap thou a twig, and thou shalt understand
    How little with thy thought the fact agrees.'

  Thereon a little I stretched forth my hand
    And plucked a tiny branch from a great thorn.
    '**Why dost thou tear me?**' made the trunk demand.

  When dark with blood it had begun to turn,
    It cried a second time: 'Why wound me thus?
    Doth not a spark of pity in thee burn?

  Though trees we be, once men were all of us;
    Yet had our souls the souls of serpents been
    Thy hand might well have proved more piteous.'

  As when the fire hath seized a fagot green
    At one extremity, the other sighs,
    And wind, escaping, hisses; so was seen,

  At where the branch was broken, blood to rise
    And words were mixed with it. I dropped the spray
    And stood like one whom terror doth surprise.

  The Sage replied: 'Soul vexed with injury,
    Had he been only able to give trust
    To what he read narrated in my lay,

  His hand toward thee would never have been thrust.
    'Tis hard for faith; and I, to make it plain,
    Urged him to trial, mourn it though I must.

  But tell him who thou wast; so shall remain
    This for amends to thee, thy fame shall blow
    Afresh on earth, where he returns again.'

  And then the trunk: 'Thy sweet words charm me so,
    I cannot dumb remain; nor count it hard
    If I some pains upon my speech bestow.

  For I am he who **held both keys in ward**
    Of Frederick's heart, and turned them how I would,
    And softly oped it, and as softly barred,

  Till scarce another in his counsel stood.
    To my high office I such loyalty bore,
    It cost me sleep and haleness of my blood.

  **The harlot** who removeth nevermore
    From Cæsar's house eyes ignorant of shame--
    A common curse, of courts the special sore--

  Set against me the minds of all aflame,
    And these in turn Augustus set on fire,
    Till my glad honours bitter woes became.

  My soul, filled full with a disdainful ire,
    Thinking by means of death disdain to flee,
    'Gainst my just self unjustly did conspire.

  I swear even by the new roots of this tree
    My fealty to my lord I never broke,
    For worthy of all honour sure was he.

  If one of you return 'mong living folk,
    Let him restore my memory, overthrown
    And suffering yet because of envy's stroke.'

  Still for a while the poet listened on,
    Then said: 'Now he is dumb, lose not the hour,
    But make request if more thou'dst have made known.'

  And I replied: 'Do thou inquire once more
    Of what thou thinkest I would gladly know;
    I cannot ask; ruth wrings me to the core.'

  On this he spake: 'Even as the man shall do,
    And liberally, what thou of him hast prayed,
    Imprisoned spirit, do thou further show

  How with these knots the spirits have been made
    Incorporate; and, if thou canst, declare
    If from such members e'er is loosed a shade.'

  Then from the trunk came vehement puffs of air;
    Next, to these words converted was the wind:
    'My answer to you shall be short and clear.

  When the fierce soul no longer is confined
    In flesh, torn thence by action of its own,
    To the Seventh Depth by Minos 'tis consigned.

  No choice is made of where it shall be thrown
    Within the wood; but where by chance 'tis flung
    It germinates like seed of spelt that's sown.

  A forest tree it grows from sapling young;
    Eating its leaves, the Harpies cause it pain,
    And open loopholes whence its sighs are wrung.

  We for our vestments shall return again
    Like others, but in them shall ne'er be clad:
    **Men justly lose what from themselves they've ta'en**.

  Dragged hither by us, all throughout the sad
    Forest our bodies shall be hung on high;
    Each on the thorn of its destructive shade.'

  While to the trunk we listening lingered nigh,
    Thinking he might proceed to tell us more,
    A sudden uproar we were startled by

  Like him who, that the huntsman and the boar
    To where he stands are sweeping in the chase,
    Knows by the crashing trees and brutish roar.

  Upon our left we saw a couple race
    Naked and scratched; and they so quickly fled
    The forest barriers burst before their face.

  'Speed to my rescue, death!' the foremost pled.
    The next, as wishing he could use more haste;
    'Not thus, O **Lano**, thee thy legs bested

  When one at Toppo's tournament thou wast.'
    Then, haply wanting breath, aside he stepped,
    Merged with a bush on which himself he cast.

  Behind them through the forest onward swept
    A pack of dogs, black, ravenous, and fleet,
    Like greyhounds from their leashes newly slipped.

  In him who crouched they made their teeth to meet,
    And, having piecemeal all his members rent,
    Haled them away enduring anguish great.

  Grasping my hand, my Escort forward went
    And led me to the bush which, all in vain,
    Through its ensanguined openings made lament.

  'James of St. Andrews,' it we heard complain;
    'What profit hadst thou making me thy shield?
    For thy bad life doth blame to me pertain?'

  Then, halting there, this speech my Master held:
    'Who wast thou that through many wounds dost sigh,
    Mingled with blood, words big with sorrow swelled?'

  'O souls that hither come,' was his reply,
    'To witness shameful outrage by me borne,
    Whence all my leaves torn off around me lie,

  Gather them to the root of this drear thorn.
    **My city for the Baptist changed of yore**
    Her former patron; wherefore, in return,

  He with his art will make her aye deplore;
    And were it not some image doth remain
    Of him where Arno's crossed from shore to shore,

  Those citizens who founded her again
    On ashes left by Attila, had spent
    Their labour of a surety all in vain.

  In my own house I up a gibbet went.'
