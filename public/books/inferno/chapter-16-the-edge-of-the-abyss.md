They know him by his clothes. In the thirteenth century dress was a civic badge: guilds, cities and ranks had their own colours and cuts, and sumptuary laws regulated who might wear what. A Florentine's gown would be recognisable even in a place without light. Dante's moving description of what he sees as they run toward him adds the other half of the picture, the scars on their limbs, both old and recent, which the flames had made. Even now my ruth is fed by memory, he says. The remark is typical of the canto, which sets reverence against condemnation. These are men of high standing in Florentine history, and the poem treats them as such, in contrast to the sneers of the previous cantos. Virgil stops to give them courtesy, noting that but for the fire it would be fitting for Dante to hurry to them rather than them to him. The first thing the dead ask is not about themselves but about home."

Guido Guerra III, Count of Modigliana (1220-1272), was a Guelph military commander whose name, meaning Guido the War, reflected his career. His grandmother Gualdrada, daughter of Bellincion Berti, was in Florentine legend a model of chastity: when Emperor Otto IV, visiting Florence, was struck by her beauty and asked that she be kissed, she answered that no man would kiss her unless he were her husband, and a count promptly asked for her hand. Dante's mention of both ancestors is a reminder of how much history could be summed up in a single family. The other two shades are Tegghiaio Aldobrandi and Jacopo Rusticucci. They are first praised with honour, and the poem's ordinary reader may not guess their sin. Dante's silence on the point makes the scene, in its extraordinary politeness, an unexpected gallery of Florentine patriots, rather than a catalogue of sinners."

Jacopo Rusticucci was a Florentine knight and wealthy merchant, a contemporary of Dante's elders. Early commentators say that he separated from his wife because of her difficult temper, and that this domestic quarrel drove him to the sin for which he now suffers. Dante's response is the real moral centre of the scene. If I had been sheltered from the fire, he says, I would have leaped down among them, nor would my Teacher have blamed me. But since I should have been baked and burned, terror prevailed the goodwill to restrain, with which to clasp them in my arms I yearned. It is a startling declaration of love for three damned men, and Dante follows it with an explanation: it was not contempt but pain which your condition in my breast awoke, because the words Virgil spoke had told him that honourable folk were approaching. Hell is a place where rank and virtue still count. The reverence of the poet for his city's heroes survives even in the circle of fire."

    content: "The three Florentines ask whether courtesy and worth still live in their city, or have been banished from it, for William Borsiere, newly arrived among them, is causing them pain with his words. Dante's answer comes in a burst, delivered with uplifted countenance: upstarts, and fortunes suddenly that grow, have bred in thee pride and extravagance, whence tears, O Florence, thou art shedding now. The Italian phrase gente nuova e i subiti guadagni means new people and quick profits. Dante is describing the social change of his own generation. In the late thirteenth century Florence was becoming one of the richest cities in Europe, thanks to wool manufacture, banking and trade, and the new money flowed to families who had no ancestral place in the civic order. Old aristocrats and new merchants competed for power, and Dante believed that this competition produced the envy and pride that ruined the city. The three shades hear the outburst as a reply, and they glance at each other as men do hearing truth. The response is almost comic: if you can satisfy others as easily, they say, you are lucky to speak so freely. Dante's judgement of his native city is delivered to its dead heroes."

    content: "As the three vanish, quicker than one could say Amen, the poets follow the stream to the place where it falls, and the roar is so loud that speech between them is nearly drowned. To describe it, Dante picks a real river: as Acquacheta in the uplands known by name, ere plunging to its bed profound, name lost ere by Forlì its waters run. The Acquacheta is a stream in the Apennines of Romagna that Dante had presumably seen on his travels. After falling in a waterfall near the abbey of San Benedetto dell'Alpe, it changes its name and becomes the Montone, flowing east toward Forlì. Dante notes that it is the first river to run to the sea from the Apennine, counting from Mount Viso in the west, an accurate piece of geography. The fall above St. Benedict is a single long bound, where for a thousand would be ample room. The reference is to the abbey, which could house a thousand monks but was inhabited by far fewer. He compares the infernal cataract, which carries the red stream of Phlegethon down into the eighth circle, with an Italian landmark that tourists could visit. Hell has its waterfalls, but the one that Dante uses to show what they sound like was in Romagna."

    wikipedia_url: "https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geryon"

    content: "At the edge of the abyss, Dante unfastens a cord that was tied about his middle, with which he once had thought that he might hold secure the leopard with the painted hide. He hands it, coiled and tight, to Virgil, who bends to the right and throws it far down into the pit. The cord is among the most puzzling props in the poem. It has been read as the girdle of the Franciscan order, which Dante may have worn briefly as a penitent, as a symbol of chastity or self-restraint, or simply as a rope. The leopard is the creature of the first canto, usually interpreted as lust or fraud. Dante thought a Franciscan cord could help him master that beast; now it serves to summon a more dangerous embodiment. The act is a good example of the poem's irony: the tool that failed on a lesser enemy is thrown away as a signal for a greater. Virgil tells Dante that what he awaits will emerge soon, quickly to thy view, that which thou dream'st of shall be clearly known. The cord has a use at last, though not the one Dante intended. The poet who has used fraud will now be used, in a way, against it."

    wikipedia_url: "https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divine_Comedy"

    content: "Before describing the monster, Dante interrupts himself: from utterance of truth which seems untrue a man should guard his tongue, lest he win blame to no transgression due. Yet now I must speak out, and by the song of this my Comedy, Reader, I swear. It is the first time in the poem that he names it. The word Commedia, in Dante's day, meant something quite specific. Classical and medieval writers distinguished between tragedy, which begins in joy and ends in sorrow, and comedy, which begins in turmoil and ends happily. Dante's poem moves from the dark wood to the vision of God, and so, by that definition, is a comedy. He also chose the title because he was writing in the humble vernacular, not in Latin, the language of high style. The adjective Divine was added only later, by Boccaccio and printers of the sixteenth century. The oath is unusual in a text where Dante almost never swears. He needs it because the next sentence asks us to believe in something that he fears will seem unbelievable: a shape, swimming upward through the air all indistinct with gross obscurity, enough to fill the stoutest heart with fear. The poem staked on its own name has to be credited."

Now could I hear the water as it fell
    To the next circle with a murmuring sound
    Like what is heard from swarming hives to swell;

  When three shades all together with a bound
    Burst from a troop met by us pressing on
    'Neath rain of that sharp torment. O'er the ground

  Toward us approaching, they exclaimed each one:
    'Halt thou, whom **from thy garb we judge** to be
    A citizen of our corrupted town.'

  Alas, what scars I on their limbs did see,
    Both old and recent, which the flames had made:
    Even now my ruth is fed by memory.

  My Teacher halted at their cry, and said:
    'Await a while:' and looked me in the face;
    'Some courtesy to these were well displayed.

  And but that fire--the manner of the place--
    Descends for ever, fitting 'twere to find
    Rather than them, thee quickening thy pace.'

  When we had halted, they again combined
    In their old song; and, reaching where we stood,
    Into a wheel all three were intertwined.

  And as the athletes used, well oiled and nude,
    To feel their grip and, wary, watch their chance,
    Ere they to purpose strike and wrestle could;

  So each of them kept fixed on me his glance
    As he wheeled round, and in opposing ways
    His neck and feet seemed ever to advance.

  'Ah, if the misery of this sand-strewn place
    Bring us and our petitions in despite,'
    One then began, 'and flayed and grimy face;

  Let at the least our fame goodwill incite
    To tell us who thou art, whose living feet
    Thus through Inferno wander without fright.

  For he whose footprints, as thou see'st, I beat,
    Though now he goes with body peeled and nude,
    More than thou thinkest, in the world was great.

  The grandson was he of Gualdrada good;
    He, **Guidoguerra**, with his armèd hand
    Did mighty things, and by his counsel shrewd.

  The other who behind me treads the sand
    Is one whose name should on the earth be dear;
    For he is Tegghiaio Aldobrand.

  And I, who am tormented with them here,
    **James Rusticucci** was; my fierce and proud
    Wife of my ruin was chief minister.'

  If from the fire there had been any shroud
    I should have leaped down 'mong them, nor have earned
    Blame, for my Teacher sure had this allowed.

  But since I should have been all baked and burned,
    Terror prevailed the goodwill to restrain
    With which to clasp them in my arms I yearned.

  Then I began: ''Twas not contempt but pain
    Which your condition in my breast awoke,
    Where deeply rooted it will long remain,

  When this my Master words unto me spoke,
    By which expectancy was in me stirred
    That ye who came were honourable folk.

  I of your city am, and with my word
    Your deeds and honoured names oft to recall
    Delighted, and with joy of them I heard.

  To the sweet fruits I go, and leave the gall,
    As promised to me by my Escort true;
    But first I to the centre down must fall.'

  'So may thy soul thy members long endue
    With vital power,' the other made reply,
    'And after thee thy fame its light renew;

  As thou shalt tell if worth and courtesy
    Within our city as of yore remain,
    Or from it have been wholly forced to fly.

  For William Borsier, one of yonder train,
    And but of late joined with us in this woe,
    Causeth us with his words exceeding pain.'

  '**Upstarts, and fortunes suddenly that grow**,
    Have bred in thee pride and extravagance,
    Whence tears, O Florence! thou art shedding now.'

  Thus cried I with uplifted countenance.
    The three, accepting it for a reply,
    Glanced each at each as hearing truth men glance.

  And all: 'If others thou shalt satisfy
    As well at other times at no more cost,
    Happy thus at thine ease the truth to cry!

  Therefore if thou escap'st these regions lost,
    Returning to behold the starlight fair,
    Then when "There was I," thou shalt make thy boast,

  Something of us do thou 'mong men declare.'
    Then broken was the wheel, and as they fled
    Their nimble legs like pinions beat the air.

  So much as one _Amen!_ had scarce been said
    Quicker than what they vanished from our view.
    On this once more the way my Master led.

  I followed, and ere long so near we drew
    To where the water fell, that for its roar
    Speech scarcely had been heard between us two.

  And as the stream which of all those which pour
    East (from Mount Viso counting) by its own
    Course falls the first from Apennine to shore--

  As **Acquacheta** in the uplands known
    By name, ere plunging to its bed profound;
    Name lost ere by Forlì its waters run--

  Above St. Benedict with one long bound,
    Where for a thousand would be ample room,
    Falls from the mountain to the lower ground;

  Down the steep cliff that water dyed in gloom
    We found to fall echoing from side to side,
    Stunning the ear with its tremendous boom.

  There was a cord about my middle tied,
    With which I once had thought that I might hold
    Secure **the leopard with the painted hide**.

  When this from round me I had quite unrolled
    To him I handed it, all coiled and tight;
    As by my Leader I had first been told.

  Himself then bending somewhat toward the right,
    He just beyond the edge of the abyss
    Threw down the cord, which disappeared from sight.

  'That some strange thing will follow upon this
    Unwonted signal which my Master's eye
    Thus follows,' so I thought, 'can hardly miss.'

  Ah, what great caution need we standing by
    Those who behold not only what is done,
    But who have wit our hidden thoughts to spy!

  He said to me: 'There shall emerge, and soon,
    What I await; and quickly to thy view
    That which thou dream'st of shall be clearly known.'

  From utterance of truth which seems untrue
    A man, whene'er he can, should guard his tongue;
    Lest he win blame to no transgression due.

  Yet now I must speak out, and by the song
    Of **this my Comedy**, Reader, I swear--
    So in good liking may it last full long!--

  I saw a shape swim upward through that air.
    All indistinct with gross obscurity,
    Enough to fill the stoutest heart with fear:

  Like one who rises having dived to free
    An anchor grappled on a jagged stone,
    Or something else deep hidden in the sea;

  With feet drawn in and arms all open thrown.
