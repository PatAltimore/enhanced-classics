    content: "Dante wakes into a rain that is heavy, cold, eternal, big with woe, and above the sodden shades howls the savage Cerberus from his threefold throat. In Greek myth Cerberus was the three-headed hound that guarded the underworld, and taming him was the twelfth labour of Heracles. Virgil mentions him in the Aeneid as a monster with a bloody maw. Dante keeps the heads but adds very human, very greasy details: oily and black his beard, red his eyes, a huge belly, and claws on his fingers. The effect is to turn the guardian into a caricature of his charges. Cerberus is gluttony made flesh, an animal that does nothing but devour and howl, and the souls beneath him are shredded in the same way a butcher treats a carcass. Their own punishment completes the picture. The gluttons lie face down in a foul slush of hail, snow and filthy water, and the stench rises from the ground. The sinners who made their appetite their god are now reduced to pigs in mud, and to dogs that yelp and shield each other. The whole circle is a joke told with a straight face: a bestial monster shrieking above bestial men."

    wikipedia_url: "https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeneid"

    content: "Cerberus opens all three mouths and shows his fangs, and Virgil does something that would make any reader laugh and shudder at once: he filled both his fists with earth and flung the load down the ravening gullets. It is a deliberate reversal of Virgil's own poem. In the sixth book of the Aeneid, the Sibyl who accompanies Aeneas silences Cerberus with a drugged honey cake. In place of a delicacy, Dante gives his guide a fistful of mud. The change is theological as well as comic. The hound is hungry, and, as sharp set with hunger barks the hound, but is appeased when at his meat he gnaws, he is satisfied by anything he can bite. Gluttony is an appetite that will take the filth it deserves. Reading allegorically, the earth is the lowest material thing, the stuff that appetite really consumes when stripped of its dressing. There is also a gentle joke at the expense of tradition: the Latin poet is made to improvise with the humblest materials instead of a magical charm. Notice that the dog's dreadful noise stopped, and the shades beneath it can hear again, though they would prefer not to: Cerberus who deafs the crowd of souls till they from hearing fain would pause."

    title: "How Dante Can Walk on the Bodies of the Dead"
    wikipedia_url: "https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul"
    image_url: ""
    image_caption: ""
    content: "As the travellers cross the circle, they step over the prone figures, treading, Dante says, upon their semblances of bodies. The phrase deserves a pause because it raises a problem the poem never fully hides. The souls in Hell have died, so they cannot have the flesh they buried. Yet they feel rain, cry out, and can be walked over. In Dante's scheme, which he explains much later through the poet Statius in Purgatory, the soul forms an airy body around itself after death, shaped like the person and carrying every sense and passion, as a rain-filled cloud takes the colours of the sun. The shade therefore has the appearance of a body without the substance, a figure that can suffer. Dante's own treatment is not always consistent. In places he treads on the shades like solid ground; elsewhere, when he tries to embrace a friend in Purgatory, his arms pass through. Here the word semblance is the safe wording. The shades look like bodies; they feel like bodies; and that appearance makes it possible to punish them. Resurrection will restore the real thing, and when it does, Virgil will say, the suffering will be sharper."

    content: "One glutton sits up in the mud and challenges Dante to recognise him. As Ciacco me your citizens named of yore, he says. The name is a Florentine nickname, usually taken to mean hog. Boccaccio's Decameron later portrays a witty Florentine parasite called Ciacco, a man who angled for dinner invitations. His real identity is lost, but his usefulness to the poem is clear. Dante asks him what will come to the divided city, and Ciacco answers with a history lesson told in the future tense. After contentious pause, blood will be spilt; the boorish party, the Whites, will chase the others out with grievous loss; then within three suns, three years, the other party will rise, holpen by him whose wiles ere now are plain. That is Pope Boniface VIII, who in 1302 backed the Blacks. Dante was a White, and the prophecy is a disguised autobiography. In 1302 he was exiled and told he would be burned alive if he returned. It is a masterstroke of structure that the poem's first political forecast comes out of a belly. The poet places the history of his own ruin in the mouth of the one man in Hell who had nothing else to do but eat, and now has nothing to say but the truth."

    trigger: "Envy, and pride, and avarice"
    title: "The Three Sparks That Dante Blamed for Florence"
    wikipedia_url: "https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seven_deadly_sins"
    image_url: ""
    image_caption: ""
    content: "Asked whether Florence holds a single just man, Ciacco answers with a bleak census: there are two just men, at whom none inquire. The sparks that set every heart on fire are envy, pride and avarice. It is a list that medieval readers would recognise, because they are three of the seven deadly sins, and Dante has already shown them in the first canto as the three beasts that drove him back from the hill: leopard, lion and wolf. Here they are applied to a city. Envy, a quality Dante and his contemporaries attributed to Florence in particular, destroys its common life; pride makes its great families fight over precedence; avarice, the trade in florins that made the city rich, turns citizens into rivals. The prophecy also records Dante's loneliness. Who are the two just men? Commentators have never agreed, and some think Dante was including himself. The line works by silence. If there are two honest men in a city of tens of thousands, and no one asks them for advice, then the political disaster that Ciacco foresees is not accident but inevitable. A republic that does not consult its just men has given up governing itself, and Dante will watch the result from outside its gates."

    content: "After the conversation, Ciacco looks at Dante with straightforward eyes, bows his head, and falls flat among the other blind. Virgil says he will not wake until he shall hear the angel's trumpet sound, ushering the hostile Judge. The image comes from the New Testament, where Paul writes of a trumpet that sounds when the dead shall be raised. For Dante and his readers the Last Judgement was a certain future event, painted on the walls of church after church. Its stakes for the Inferno are important. At the trumpet, each shade shall find its dismal sepulchre, resume its flesh and ancient form, and listen to what echoes in eternal round, the sentence, forever. The souls of Hell are living in an interim state, and a second, heavier verdict is still to come. The Judge is called hostile because, for the damned, Christ at the Last Judgement is no consoler. Dante returns to the image in Canto 10, where the heretics lie in tombs that will be closed for ever on the day of judgement. Virgil's description of a final resurrection lets the poem end its first long episode on this circle with a promise that nothing about Hell is temporary."

    title: "The Philosophy That Makes Hell Worse After the Resurrection"
    wikipedia_url: "https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotle"
    image_url: ""
    image_caption: ""
    content: "Dante, still thinking about the last trumpet, asks whether torments will grow, or lesser prove and not so fiercely glow, after the sentence has been heard. Virgil does not reassure him. Repair unto thy Science, he says, meaning the philosophy of Aristotle as taught in the medieval schools. The principle runs that the more perfect a thing is, the more keenly it feels, whether pleasure or pain. A soul alone is a partial thing; a soul reunited with its body is complete. After the Resurrection, the damned will be nearer perfection than they are now, though never fully perfect, since that is denied them. They will therefore feel more, not less. It is a chilling application of a neutral doctrine. The same idea is used in Paradise, where Dante explains that the blessed will be even happier after they recover their bodies, and Aquinas builds his own account of the resurrection on it. Virgil's phrasing is a small act of literary tact. He does not offer a lecture; he sends the pupil back to his notes. The effect is that Hell becomes less a nightmare than a logical consequence of the same metaphysics that supports Heaven, and that makes it harder to look away from."

When I regained my senses, which had fled
    At my compassion for the kindred two,
    Which for pure sorrow quite had turned my head,

  New torments and a crowd of sufferers new
    I see around me as I move again,
    Where'er I turn, where'er I bend my view.

  In the Third Circle am I of the rain
    Which, heavy, cold, eternal, big with woe,
    Doth always of one kind and force remain.

  Large hail and turbid water, mixed with snow,
    Keep pouring down athwart the murky air;
    And from the ground they fall on, stenches grow.

  The savage **Cerberus**, a monster drear,
    Howls from his threefold throat with canine cries
    Above the people who are whelmèd there.

  Oily and black his beard, and red his eyes,
    His belly huge: claws from his fingers sprout.
    The shades he flays, hooks, rends in cruel wise.

  Beat by the rain these, dog-like, yelp and shout,
    And shield themselves in turn with either side;
    And oft the wretched sinners turn about.

  When we by Cerberus, great worm, were spied,
    He oped his mouths and all his fangs he showed,
    While not a limb did motionless abide.

  My Leader having spread his hands abroad,
    **Filled both his fists with earth** ta'en from the ground,
    And down the ravening gullets flung the load.

  Then, as sharp set with hunger barks the hound,
    But is appeased when at his meat he gnaws,
    And, worrying it, forgets all else around;

  So with those filthy faces there it was
    Of the fiend Cerberus, who deafs the crowd
    Of souls till they from hearing fain would pause.

  We, travelling o'er the spirits who lay cowed
    And sorely by the grievous showers harassed,
    Upon their **semblances of bodies** trod.

  Prone on the ground the whole of them were cast,
    Save one of them who sat upright with speed
    When he beheld that near to him we passed.

  'O thou who art through this Inferno led,
    Me if thou canst,' he asked me, 'recognise;
    For ere I was dismantled thou wast made.'

  And I to him: 'Thy present tortured guise
    Perchance hath blurred my memory of thy face,
    Until it seems I ne'er on thee set eyes.

  But tell me who thou art, within this place
    So cruel set, exposed to such a pain,
    Than which, if greater, none has more disgrace.'

  And he: 'Thy city, swelling with the bane
    Of envy till the sack is running o'er,
    Me in the life serene did once contain.

  As **Ciacco** me your citizens named of yore;
    And for the damning sin of gluttony
    I, as thou seest, am beaten by this shower.

  No solitary woful soul am I,
    For all of these endure the selfsame doom
    For the same fault.' Here ended his reply.

  I answered him, 'O Ciacco, with such gloom
    Thy misery weighs me, I to weep am prone;
    But, if thou canst, declare to what shall come

  The citizens of the divided town.
    Holds it one just man? And declare the cause
    Why 'tis of discord such a victim grown.'

  Then he to me: 'After contentious pause
    Blood will be spilt; the boorish party then
    Will chase the others forth with grievous loss.

  The former it behoves to fall again
    Within three suns, the others to ascend,
    Holpen by him whose wiles ere now are plain.

  Long time, with heads held high, they'll make to bend
    The other party under burdens dire,
    Howe'er themselves in tears and rage they spend.

  There are two just men, at whom none inquire.
    **Envy, and pride, and avarice**, even these
    Are the three sparks have set all hearts on fire.'

  With this the tearful sound he made to cease:
    And I to him, 'Yet would I have thee tell--
    And of thy speech do thou the gift increase--

  Tegghiaio and Farinata, honourable,
    James Rusticucci, Mosca, Arrigo,
    With all the rest so studious to excel

  In good; where are they? Help me this to know;
    Great hunger for the news hath seizèd me;
    Delights them Heaven, or tortures Hell below?'

  He said: 'Among the blackest souls they be;
    Them to the bottom weighs another sin.
    Shouldst thou so far descend, thou mayst them see.

  But when the sweet world thou again dost win,
    I pray thee bring me among men to mind;
    No more I tell, nor new reply begin.'

  Then his straightforward eyes askance declined;
    He looked at me a moment ere his head
    He bowed; then fell flat 'mong the other blind.

  'Henceforth he waketh not,' my Leader said,
    'Till he shall hear **the angel's trumpet sound**,
    Ushering the hostile Judge. By every shade

  Its dismal sepulchre shall then be found,
    Its flesh and ancient form it shall resume,
    And list what echoes in eternal round.'

  So passed we where the shades and rainy spume
    Made filthy mixture, with steps taken slow;
    Touching a little on the world to come.

  Wherefore I said: 'Master, shall torments grow
    After the awful sentence hath been heard,
    Or lesser prove and not so fiercely glow?'

  '**Repair unto thy Science**,' was his word;
    'Which tells, as things approach a perfect state
    To keener joy or suffering they are stirred.

  Therefore although this people cursed by fate
    Ne'er find perfection in its full extent,
    To it they then shall more approximate

  Than now.' Our course we round the circle bent,
    Still holding speech, of which I nothing say,
    Until we came where down the pathway went:

  There found we Plutus, the great enemy.
