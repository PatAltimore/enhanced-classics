    wikipedia_url: "https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malebolge"
    image_url: ""

    content: "The canto opens with architecture. There is a place in Hell called Malebolge, of iron colour and composed of stone, girt by a cliff of the same substance. In its centre yawns a well, ample and profound, and the zone between well and cliff is round, divided into ten vales. The word is Dante's own coinage, from the Italian male, bad, and bolge, pouches or pockets, so the name means evil pouches. The picture he draws is of a castle: as numerous moats a castle's towers enclose, that they the walls may better fortify, so here ridges run from the cliff's base across the ditches, like drawbridges across the moats, until they join at the well. The geometry is one of the most precise in the Inferno. Each ditch, or bolgia, holds a particular kind of fraud, so the eighth circle is a catalogue of lying: seducers, flatterers, simoniacs, sorcerers, barrators, hypocrites, thieves, evil counsellors, sowers of discord and falsifiers. The scale is bureaucratic. Fraud, unlike violence, is not one sin but a whole profession with divisions, and the ten pouches give the reader a map of how many ways human beings can betray trust. The well in the middle leads further down to the final circle of traitors."

    content: "In the first ditch, naked sinners march in two lines, one facing the poets, the other going the same way but faster, while horned devils whip them from behind. To explain the pattern, Dante recalls a rule from a recent event: even so the Romans, that the mighty crowd across the bridge, the year of Jubilee, might pass with ease, ordained a rule of road. Those facing the Castle went toward St. Peter's, and those facing the Mount went the other way. The Jubilee of 1300 was the first in Christian history, proclaimed by Pope Boniface VIII, who promised full forgiveness of sins to pilgrims who visited the basilicas of Rome. Chroniclers estimated hundreds of thousands of visitors, and Dante, who is placing his journey in that very year, may have crossed the Ponte Sant'Angelo himself. The Castle is Castel Sant'Angelo, and the Mount is Monte Giordano. By borrowing an image of orderly pilgrims for a procession of the damned, he makes the connection between holy and unholy crowds explicit. Both go in one direction, in one file, under compulsion. It is no accident that the moral scandal of Boniface will be raised in the very next canto."

His sister Ghisolabella was said to have been procured for the Marquis Obizzo d'Este of Ferrara, a notorious lecher, and Venedico himself, according to early commentators, had arranged it for money. Sipa is the Bolognese word for yes, in the local dialect, and the rivers Reno and Savena mark the edges of the city. Dante is claiming that there are now more Bolognese in this one pit than are living in their hometown, on account of their greed. A demon shouts pander, begone, here are no women for thy coin to buy, and cracks his whip. Dante's contempt is plain, but so is his curiosity: the exchange is a study in how shame works. The man tries to turn away because he knows that to be seen is already to be punished."

    content: "After the encounter with Venedico, the poets climb a rib of rock that spans the ditch. Virgil then tells Dante to stop and look down at the other file of sinners, whose faces he has not yet seen because they walk in the same direction as the travellers. Then from the ancient bridge by us were seen the troop which toward us on that circuit sped. The bridge is one of the stone ridges that run across the ten ditches like drawbridges, joining the outer cliff to the central well, and its antiquity matters. Dante will explain in Canto 21 that the bridges are broken in places, because the earthquake that shook Hell at the death of Christ cracked the rock, a detail that explains why parts of the road are missing and why the travellers must sometimes climb down. The detail has a double edge. A scar from the most important event in Christian history is the very path by which Dante is being led, so the pilgrimage is conducted over a monument to the Redemption. It also builds a sense of geological time. Hell has been here, and been damaged, and rebuilt, over a thousand years. Dante's tour of the fraudulent is happening on structures that long predate its inhabitants."

On the way he stopped on Lemnos, an island whose women had murdered their husbands, and seduced the island's queen Hypsipyle, who was pregnant when he abandoned her. Later in Colchis he won the sorceress Medea, who helped him steal the fleece and killed her own brother to cover their escape, and then he abandoned her too, in favour of a Corinthian princess. The story is from Ovid and Statius, and its moral is that Jason's heroism does not exempt him. His proud bearing is typical of the poem's treatment of great men. Dante will return to Jason in the nineteenth canto as a type of the corrupt priest, and in the last cantos of Paradise as the image of the poet who sees the Argo's shadow in his own voyage."

The man strikes his own head and gives his reason: 'twas flattery steeped me here; for, using such, my tongue itself enough could never please. Alessio Interminei came from a noble family of Lucca, and the commentators who knew him say that his flattery was proverbial. The confession is a perfect piece of self-reproach, because it admits that flattery has no natural limit: a man who flatters cannot stop. The punishment is a pun on the language itself. The words of flatterers were smooth and sweet, and in Hell the substance they most resemble is also what they were worth. Dante's coarse imagery is deliberate. After a canto of elegant fraud, the second ditch reduces honeyed speech to ordure. The poet saves his most disgusted language for the sin that seems, in life, the most harmless."

'Tis harlot Thais, who, when she had been asked by her lover, Am I generous and worthy thanks? said, Greatly so, I ween. The exchange comes from the Eunuchus, a comedy by the Roman playwright Terence. In the play, the soldier Thraso sends Thais a present and asks the messenger whether she was grateful, and the parasite Gnatho answers that she thanked him enormously. Dante did not read Terence directly; he took the example from Cicero's De Amicitia, which cites the scene as a model of flattery, in which the courtier replies with an exaggeration to please his master. Dante makes a subtle swap: in the play it is the parasite who flatters, and here it is the courtesan, the figure of the story, who answers. The substitution reflects his taste for compression. He needs one image to sum up the whole ditch, and a woman who says whatever the man wants to hear fits. Virgil's closing words, enough of this place has been seen by us, are an unusual note of disgust from the poem's most patient guide."

Of iron colour, and composed of stone,
    A place called **Malebolge** is in Hell,
    Girt by a cliff of substance like its own.

  In that malignant region yawns a well
    Right in the centre, ample and profound;
    Of which I duly will the structure tell.

  The zone that lies between them, then, is round--
    Between the well and precipice hard and high;
    Into ten vales divided is the ground.

  As is the figure offered to the eye,
    Where numerous moats a castle's towers enclose
    That they the walls may better fortify;

  A like appearance was made here by those.
    And as, again, from threshold of such place
    Many a drawbridge to the outworks goes;

  So ridges from the precipice's base
    Cutting athwart the moats and barriers run,
    Till at the well join the extremities.

  From Geryon's back when we were shaken down
    'Twas here we stood, until the Poet's feet
    Moved to the left, and I, behind, came on.

  New torments on the right mine eyes did meet
    With new tormentors, novel woe on woe;
    With which the nearer Bolgia was replete.

  Sinners, all naked, in the gulf below,
    This side the middle met us; while they strode
    On that side with us, but more swift did go.

  Even so the Romans, that the mighty crowd
    Across the bridge, **the year of Jubilee**,
    Might pass with ease, ordained a rule of road--

  Facing the Castle, on that side should be
    The multitude which to St. Peter's hied;
    So to the Mount on this was passage free.

  On the grim rocky ground, on either side,
    I saw horned devils armed with heavy whip
    Which on the sinners from behind they plied.

  Ah, how they made the wretches nimbly skip
    At the first lashes; no one ever yet
    But sought from the second and the third to slip.

  And as I onward went, mine eyes were set
    On one of them; whereon I called in haste:
    'This one already I have surely met!'

  Therefore to know him, fixedly I gazed;
    And my kind Leader willingly delayed,
    While for a little I my course retraced.

  On this the scourged one, thinking to evade
    My search, his visage bent without avail,
    For: 'Thou that gazest on the ground,' I said,

  'If these thy features tell trustworthy tale,
    **Venedico Caccianimico** thou!
    But what has brought thee to such sharp regale?'

  And he, 'I tell it 'gainst my will, I trow,
    But thy clear accents to the old world bear
    My memory, and make me all avow.

  I was the man who Ghisola the fair
    To serve the Marquis' evil will led on,
    Whatever the uncomely tale declare.

  Of Bolognese here weeping not alone
    Am I; so full the place of them, to-day
    'Tween Reno and Savena are not known

  So many tongues that _Sipa_ deftly say:
    And if of this thou'dst know the reason why,
    Think but how greedy were our hearts alway.'

  To him thus speaking did a demon cry:
    'Pander, begone!' and smote him with his thong;
    'Here are no women for thy coin to buy.'

  Then, with my Escort joined, I moved along.
    Few steps we made until we there had come,
    Where from the bank a rib of rock was flung.

  With ease enough up to its top we clomb,
    And, turning on the ridge, bore to the right;
    And those eternal circles parted from.

  When we had reached where underneath the height
    A passage opes, yielding the scourged a way,
    My Guide bade: 'Tarry, so to hold in sight

  Those other spirits born in evil day,
    Whose faces until now from thee have been
    Concealed, because with ours their progress lay.'

  Then from the **ancient bridge** by us were seen
    The troop which toward us on that circuit sped,
    Chased onward, likewise, by the scourges keen.

  And my good Master, ere I asked him, said:
    'That lordly one now coming hither, see,
    By whom, despite of pain, no tears are shed.

  What mien he still retains of majesty!
    'Tis **Jason**, who by courage and by guile
    The Colchians of the ram deprived. 'Twas he

  Who on his passage by the Lemnian isle,
    Where all of womankind with daring hand
    Upon their males had wrought a murder vile,

  With loving pledges and with speeches bland
    The tender-yeared Hypsipyle betrayed,
    Who had herself a fraud on others planned.

  Forlorn he left her then, when pregnant made.
    That is the crime condemns him to this pain;
    And for Medea too is vengeance paid.

  Who in his manner cheat compose his train.
    Of the first moat sufficient now is known,
    And those who in its jaws engulfed remain.'

  Already had we by the strait path gone
    To where 'tis with the second bank dovetailed--
    The buttress whence a second arch is thrown.

  Here heard we who in the next Bolgia wailed
    And puffed for breath; reverberations told
    They with their open palms themselves assailed.

  The sides were crusted over with a mould
    Plastered upon them by foul mists that rise,
    And both with eyes and nose a contest hold.

  The bottom is so deep, in vain our eyes
    Searched it till further up the bridge we went,
    To where the arch o'erhangs what under lies.

  Ascended there, our eyes we downward bent,
    And I saw people in such ordure drowned,
    A very cesspool 'twas of excrement.

  And while I from above am searching round,
    One with a head so filth-smeared I picked out,
    I knew not if 'twas lay, or tonsure-crowned.

  'Why then so eager,' asked he with a shout,
    'To stare at me of all the filthy crew?'
    And I to him: 'Because I scarce can doubt

  That formerly thee dry of hair I knew,
    **Alessio Interminei** the Lucchese;
    And therefore thee I chiefly hold in view.'

  Smiting his head-piece, then, his words were these:
    ''Twas flattery steeped me here; for, using such,
    My tongue itself enough could never please.'

  'Now stretch thou somewhat forward, but not much,'
    Thereon my Leader bade me, 'and thine eyes
    Slowly advance till they her features touch

  And the dishevelled baggage recognise,
    Clawing her yonder with her nails unclean,
    Now standing up, now squatting on her thighs.

  'Tis **harlot Thais**, who, when she had been
    Asked by her lover, "Am I generous
    And worthy thanks?" said, "Greatly so, I ween."

  Enough of this place has been seen by us.'
