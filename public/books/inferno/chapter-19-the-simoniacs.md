    content: "The nineteenth canto opens with a curse on Simon Magus and his wretched crew, who prostitute the gifts of God to gold and silver. Simon Magus, in the Acts of the Apostles, offered the apostles money for the power to confer the Holy Spirit, and from his name the sale of church offices became known as simony. Then Dante turns to the third ditch, whose livid stone is pierced with round holes of equal size. They are no larger nor less wide, he says, than those within my beautiful St. John for the baptizers' standing-place supplied. St. John is the Baptistery of Florence, the octagonal church where every Florentine child was baptised and the one that Dante calls my beautiful. The holes in question are small wells in the floor around the font where the priests stood. Dante then adds an astonishing aside: one of them, not many years agone, I broke to save one drowning, and he asks us to take this as a seal to undeceive men. Someone had accused him of vandalism. The little anecdote, which no other source confirms in detail, shows his habit of defending his reputation in his poem and reminds us that this is a Florentine's hell, built from the sacred places he knew from childhood."

    content: "From the hole, an upside-down sinner cries out as Dante approaches: art thou arrived already, art thou arrived already, Boniface? By several years the prophecy has lied. He is mistaking the pilgrim for Pope Boniface VIII, who in the poem's year of 1300 is still alive, sitting on the throne, and whom Dante held responsible for his exile and for the corruption of the Church. The speaker, who thinks Boniface has come early, is revealed in the next lines as Pope Nicholas III (Giovanni Gaetano Orsini), who reigned from 1277 to 1280. Nicholas's confusion is caused by his place in the hole: the simoniac popes are stacked one on top of another, each pressed into the rock by the arrival of the next. Nicholas is waiting for Boniface; a prophecy, he says, had told him the time. The tone is pitiless comedy. Dante's reaction is to stand like a friar who hears the last confession of an assassin, who is buried alive, and calls the confessor back to delay the death. He is also nonplussed: he does not understand what he is being told, until Virgil whispers the reply: say, I am not he thou thinkest, no, not he! The poem reads the future of a living pope out of the dead one's mouth."

    content: "Nicholas turns his twisting feet and answers sighing, in a voice of deep distress: know, the Great Mantle sometime was my dress. I of the Bear, in sooth, was worthy son. The Great Mantle is the papal robe, the red cope of the pope, and the bear is the Orsini family, whose name comes from orso, a bear. The pun runs further: as once, the Cubs to help, my purse with gain I stuffed, myself I in this purse have stown. Nicholas had promoted his relatives, the cubs, to cardinals, governorships and large estates, and Dante accuses him of filling his purse in order to enrich them. The word stown describes a hole, a pocket: he is now in the purse he made. Contemporary chroniclers accused Nicholas III of just this nepotism, and of accepting bribes, including a reputed gift from the Byzantine emperor Michael VIII Palaiologos. Under him, the Orsini family became one of the most powerful in Rome. The last sentence adds a final humiliation. Under his head lie stretched out the simoniacs that went before him, flattened throughout the rocky vein. The papal line has become a geological stratum, each pope pressing the previous one lower into the stone."

    content: "Nicholas predicts that after Boniface will come one viler still, a Pastor from the West, lawless of deed: a modern Jason he, of whom we read in Maccabees, whose King denied him nought; with the French King so shall this man succeed. The new pope is Clement V, a Gascon elected in 1305, who moved the papacy from Rome to Avignon in 1309 and remained subservient to Philip IV of France. Dante's comparison reaches into the second book of the Maccabees, which tells how a man named Jason bought the office of high priest from the Seleucid king Antiochus IV and brought Greek customs into Jerusalem. The first Jason in Dante's poem, the Greek hero, was a seducer; the second, a biblical one, is a buyer of priesthood, and Clement matches him by trading the Church's independence for royal favour. It is an apt echo, because Nicholas's own name, Orsini, stands for authority rooted in Rome, while Clement stands for exile on the Rhône. Dante, writing after 1309, is composing a prophecy that was already history, and the date helps place the passage: this canto was written by a poet who had seen the papal court cross the Alps and who regarded the move as the culmination of simony."

    wikipedia_url: "https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whore_of_Babylon"

    image_caption: ""
    content: "Dante cannot restrain himself. After asking Nicholas what price Christ asked of Peter when he gave him the keys, he turns on the popes in a sustained denunciation. It was you, O pastors, the Evangelist bore in mind what time he saw her on the flood of waters set, who played with kings the whore; who with seven heads was born, and as she would, by the ten horns to her was service done, long as her spouse rejoiced in what was good. The image comes from the Book of Revelation, where John sees a woman seated on a scarlet beast with seven heads and ten horns, drunk with the blood of the saints, called Babylon the Great, mother of harlots. Medieval readers usually interpreted her as pagan Rome, but Dante reads her as the corrupt Church, once the bride of Christ and faithful as long as her husband rejoiced in virtue, the seven heads being the sacraments or gifts of the Spirit, the ten horns the commandments. The passage goes on: now gold and silver are your god alone; what difference twixt the idolater and you, save that ye pray a hundred for his one? The accusation is not a general complaint but a charge of idolatry, delivered by a lay poet to the rulers of Christendom."

    wikipedia_url: "https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donation_of_Constantine"
    image_url: ""
    image_caption: ""
    content: "The canto ends with a cry that echoes through the rest of the Divine Comedy: Ah, Constantine, how many evils grew, not from thy change of faith, but from the gift wherewith thou didst the first rich Pope endue! He refers to the Donation of Constantine, a document believed in the Middle Ages to have been issued by the Roman emperor Constantine the Great in the early fourth century. It purported to grant Pope Sylvester I and his successors authority over Rome, the western provinces and other territories, together with the imperial insignia. The Church used it for centuries to claim temporal power. Dante accepted its authenticity but thought it a disaster, because it gave the Church wealth and political rule, which he considered incompatible with its spiritual mission. He makes the argument at length in his treatise on monarchy. The irony is that the document was a forgery, probably composed in the eighth century, and it was exposed in 1440 by the humanist Lorenzo Valla. Dante's line is therefore based on a false premise, but its emotional logic stands: a church enriched by a gift turns from the poor fisherman to a rich lord. As the pope twists his soles in rage or conscience, Virgil listens with lips so well content, and carries Dante up to the next bridge."

O Simon Magus! ye his wretched crew!
    The gifts of God, ordained to be the bride
    Of righteousness, ye prostitute that you

  With gold and silver may be satisfied;
    Therefore for you let now the trumpet blow,
    Seeing that ye in the Third Bolgia 'bide.

  Arrived at the next tomb, we to the brow
    Of rock ere this had finished our ascent,
    Which hangs true plumb above the pit below.

  What perfect art, O Thou Omniscient,
    Is Thine in Heaven and earth and the bad world found!
    How justly does Thy power its dooms invent!

  The livid stone, on both banks and the ground,
    I saw was full of holes on every side,
    All of one size, and each of them was round.

  No larger seemed they to me nor less wide
    Than those within **my beautiful St. John**
    For the baptizers' standing-place supplied;

  And one of which, not many years agone,
    I broke to save one drowning; and I would
    Have this for seal to undeceive men known.

  Out of the mouth of each were seen protrude
    A sinner's feet, and of the legs the small
    Far as the calves; the rest enveloped stood.

  And set on fire were both the soles of all,
    Which made their ankles wriggle with such throes
    As had made ropes and withes asunder fall.

  And as **flame fed by unctuous matter** goes
    Over the outer surface only spread;
    So from their heels it flickered to the toes.

  'Master, who is he, tortured more,' I said,
    'Than are his neighbours, writhing in such woe;
    And licked by flames of deeper-hearted red?'

  And he: 'If thou desirest that below
    I bear thee by that bank which lowest lies,
    Thou from himself his sins and name shalt know.'

  And I: 'Thy wishes still for me suffice:
    Thou art my Lord, and knowest I obey
    Thy will; and dost my hidden thoughts surprise.'

  To the fourth barrier then we made our way,
    And, to the left hand turning, downward went
    Into the narrow hole-pierced cavity;

  Nor the good Master caused me make descent
    From off his haunch till we his hole were nigh
    Who with his shanks was making such lament.

  'Whoe'er thou art, soul full of misery,
    Set like a stake with lower end upcast,'
    I said to him, 'Make, if thou canst, reply.'

  I like a friar stood who gives the last
    Shrift to a vile assassin, to his side
    Called back to win delay for him fixed fast.

  'Art thou arrived already?' then he cried,
    'Art thou arrived already, **Boniface**?
    By several years the prophecy has lied.

  Art so soon wearied of the wealthy place,
    For which thou didst not fear to take with guile,
    Then ruin the fair Lady?' Now my case

  Was like to theirs who linger on, the while
    They cannot comprehend what they are told,
    And as befooled from further speech resile.

  But Virgil bade me: 'Speak out loud and bold,
    "I am not he thou thinkest, no, not he!"'
    And I made answer as by him controlled.

  The spirit's feet then twisted violently,
    And, sighing in a voice of deep distress,
    He asked: 'What then requirest thou of me?

  If me to know thou hast such eagerness,
    That thou the cliff hast therefore ventured down,
    Know, **the Great Mantle** sometime was my dress.

  I of the Bear, in sooth, was worthy son:
    As once, the Cubs to help, my purse with gain
    I stuffed, myself I in this purse have stown.

  Stretched out at length beneath my head remain
    All the simoniacs that before me went,
    And flattened lie throughout the rocky vein.

  I in my turn shall also make descent,
    Soon as he comes who I believed thou wast,
    When I asked quickly what for him was meant.

  O'er me with blazing feet more time has past,
    While upside down I fill the topmost room,
    Than he his crimsoned feet shall upward cast;

  For after him one viler still shall come,
    A Pastor from the West, lawless of deed:
    To cover both of us his worthy doom.

  **A modern Jason** he, of whom we read
    In Maccabees, whose King denied him nought:
    With the French King so shall this man succeed.'

  Perchance I ventured further than I ought,
    But I spake to him in this measure free:
    'Ah, tell me now what money was there sought

  Of Peter by our Lord, when either key
    He gave him in his guardianship to hold?
    Sure He demanded nought save: "Follow me!"

  Nor Peter, nor the others, asked for gold
    Or silver when upon Matthias fell
    The lot instead of him, the traitor-souled.

  Keep then thy place, for thou art punished well,
    And clutch the pelf, dishonourably gained,
    Which against Charles made thee so proudly swell.

  And, were it not that I am still restrained
    By reverence for those tremendous keys,
    Borne by thee while the glad world thee contained,

  I would use words even heavier than these;
    Seeing your avarice makes the world deplore,
    Crushing the good, filling the bad with ease.

  'Twas you, O Pastors, the Evangelist bore
    In mind what time he saw her on the flood
    Of waters set, who **played with kings the whore**;

  Who with seven heads was born; and as she would
    By the ten horns to her was service done,
    Long as her spouse rejoiced in what was good.

  Now gold and silver are your god alone:
    What difference 'twixt the idolater and you,
    Save that ye pray a hundred for his one?

  **Ah, Constantine**, how many evils grew--
    Not from thy change of faith, but from the gift
    Wherewith thou didst the first rich Pope endue!'

  While I my voice continued to uplift
    To such a tune, by rage or conscience stirred
    Both of his soles he made to twist and shift.

  My Guide, I well believe, with pleasure heard;
    Listening he stood with lips so well content
    To me propounding truthful word on word.

  Then round my body both his arms he bent,
    And, having raised me well upon his breast,
    Climbed up the path by which he made descent.

  Nor was he by his burden so oppressed
    But that he bore me to the bridge's crown,
    Which with the fourth joins the fifth rampart's crest.

  And lightly here he set his burden down,
    Found light by him upon the precipice,
    Up which a goat uneasily had gone.

  And thence another valley met mine eyes.
